AI Industry Chain Encyclopedia

A calm field guide to the stack

Many people first encounter the AI industry squeezed between two voices: one that says “everything will be disrupted,” and another that throws out opaque acronyms—HBM, CoWoS, MFU, KV Cache. This long-form guide tries something simpler: lay the chain out so you can see where silicon comes from, where power flows, how models get trained, and where value is ultimately settled.

Think of it as a map for engineers, product people, and researchers—not a press release. You do not need to read it in one sitting; come back to look up terms, fix your coordinates, and pressure-test your own judgments.

How to read this more easily

Spend ten-odd minutes on the panorama first and pin upstream–midstream–downstream in your head. Then dig only into the link that actually blocks you—maybe why HBM is expensive, or why training burns so much power. Use the sidebar to jump and the header to search. Company roles here reflect public industry consensus for orientation only; this is not investment advice.

2. Core Glossary

In industry discussions, half the confusion comes from “the same word naming different layers.” This glossary is not exhaustive; it aims to be enough, clear, and look-up friendly. Use it as a dictionary: when a later section drops an unfamiliar acronym, come back for a minute or two.

Artificial Intelligence AI Fundamentals

A family of techniques that enable machines to perform tasks that usually require human intelligence—perception, reasoning, learning, decision-making, and generation. In today’s industry context, AI often specifically means systems based on machine learning, especially deep learning and large-scale pre-trained models.

Machine Learning ML Fundamentals

An algorithmic paradigm that improves performance automatically from data. Models estimate mappings from samples rather than being fully hand-coded with rules. Supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning are common branches.

Deep Learning DL Fundamentals

Machine learning that uses multi-layer neural networks for representation learning. Deep nets can extract hierarchical features automatically and underpin modern vision, speech, and large language models.

Generative AI GenAI Fundamentals

AI systems whose primary capability is generating new content—text, images, audio, code, video, and more—as opposed to purely discriminative models that only classify or detect. LLMs and diffusion models are representative architectures.

Discriminative AI Fundamentals

Systems focused on judgment and classification (spam detection, image labels), modeling the conditional distribution P(y|x). Contrasts with generative modeling of P(x) or P(x,y). Still widely used in industrial inspection and risk control.

Neural Network NN Fundamentals

A differentiable computational graph of interconnected “neuron” layers. Weights are updated via backpropagation and gradient descent. Transformers, CNNs, and RNNs are structural variants.

Compute Computing power Industry

The total computational resources needed for AI training and inference, often measured in FLOPS, GPU-hours, or cluster scale. Compute is one of the hardest constraints on AI industrialization, tightly coupled to chips, power, and interconnect.

Scaling Laws Theory

Empirical regularities describing how model performance improves predictably as parameters, data, and compute grow. They help explain the industry’s push toward larger models and more data, while also inviting debate about data exhaustion and diminishing returns.

Large Language Model LLM Model

A language model typically with billions of parameters that predicts the next token autoregressively. After large-scale text pre-training, it can be adapted via instruction tuning and alignment for dialogue, reasoning, coding, and more.

Foundation Model Model

A general-purpose model pre-trained on broad data and adaptable to many downstream tasks. LLMs and multimodal models fall here, as opposed to narrow models trained for a single task.

Transformer Architecture

A sequence-modeling architecture proposed in 2017 based on self-attention. Highly parallelizable, it is the de facto backbone of contemporary LLMs and most multimodal models.

Parameter Model

A learnable weight in the model. Parameter count affects capacity, memory footprint, and inference cost. “70B” means roughly 70 billion parameters; quantization can reduce storage and bandwidth needs.

Token Data

The basic unit of text a model processes, usually a subword. Billing, context length, and training data scale are often measured in tokens. Tokenization efficiency differs across languages.

Context Window Model

The maximum number of tokens a model can “see” at once. Longer context helps document analysis and multi-turn dialogue, but attention compute and KV Cache memory rise sharply with length.

Pre-training Training

Learning general representations on massive unlabeled or weakly labeled corpora, usually via next-token prediction. Consumes most of the compute and data budget and produces a foundation checkpoint.

Fine-tuning Training

Continued training of a pre-trained model on task data to fit a domain or format. Includes full-parameter fine-tuning and parameter-efficient methods (LoRA, QLoRA, adapters, and so on).

Alignment Training

Making model outputs match human intent, values, and safety constraints. Common techniques: SFT (supervised fine-tuning), RLHF, DPO, Constitutional AI, and related methods.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation RAG System

Retrieving relevant snippets from an external knowledge base and injecting them into context before generation, to reduce hallucinations, refresh knowledge, and support private data. Vector databases and embedding models are companion components.

Agent System

A system that can plan steps, call tools, interact with an environment, and iterate toward a goal. The LLM supplies reasoning and a language interface; tool use, memory, and workflow orchestration are core engineering modules.

Multimodal Model

Models and systems that process or generate multiple modalities (text, image, audio, video, and so on). Vision-language models (VLMs) are a typical example.

Hallucination Risk

When a model produces content that sounds plausible but is factually wrong or fabricated. In high-stakes decisions it must be mitigated with RAG, tool verification, human review, and similar controls.

Chain-of-Thought CoT Reasoning

Prompting the model to write intermediate reasoning steps to improve accuracy on hard tasks. Related ideas include tree search, process reward models (PRMs), and test-time compute.

Distillation Training

Training a smaller student model from a larger teacher’s outputs or representations to keep much of the capability at lower deployment cost. Widely used for on-device and high-throughput inference.

Quantization Inference

Compressing weights/activations from high precision (FP16/BF16) to lower bit-widths (INT8, INT4, FP8, and so on) to cut memory and raise throughput, usually with controlled accuracy loss.

GPU Graphics Processing Unit Chip

A highly parallel processor originally for graphics, now the main general-purpose accelerator for AI training and inference. NVIDIA’s CUDA ecosystem is one of the most complete software stacks today.

TPU Tensor Processing Unit Chip

Google’s custom AI accelerator optimized for tensor math, used mainly on Google Cloud and internal training/inference—an example of the “hyperscaler custom ASIC” path.

ASIC Application-Specific Integrated Circuit Chip

A chip tailored to a specific algorithm or workload. Relative to GPUs it can win on energy efficiency and cost, at the price of flexibility and longer design cycles. Training and inference ASICs target different goals.

NPU Neural Processing Unit Chip

A processor aimed at neural-network acceleration, common in phone SoCs, PCs, and edge devices. Emphasizes efficiency and always-on AI tasks; peak compute is usually far below data-center GPUs.

CPU Central Processing Unit Chip

The general-purpose control and serial-work core. In AI servers it handles scheduling, data prep, networking, and non-GPU-friendly ops; training clusters still need strong CPUs and ample host memory.

FPGA Field-Programmable Gate Array Chip

Reconfigurable logic suited to custom pipelines and low-latency niches. In AI it appears more in specialized inference, network offload, or prototyping than as a mainstream accelerator versus GPU/ASIC.

Training Workload

Updating model parameters via forward/backward passes. Traits: high compute, high communication bandwidth, high HBM demand, long-running jobs, and critical fault tolerance and checkpointing.

Inference Workload

Using a trained model to produce predictions or outputs. Online inference cares about latency and throughput; offline batch inference cares about cost. KV Cache, batching, and speculative decoding are key optimizations.

CUDA Software stack

NVIDIA’s parallel computing platform and API. Together with cuDNN, NCCL, TensorRT, and more it forms a deep moat. Compatibility and ecosystem stickiness heavily influence GPU selection.

Tensor Core Hardware

Specialized GPU execution units optimized for matrix multiply-add (MMA), supporting mixed precision (TF32, BF16, FP8, and so on)—central to modern AI GPU performance jumps.

Advanced Packaging Manufacturing

Technologies such as CoWoS, SoIC, and EMIB that densely interconnect logic dies with HBM and similar parts. Advanced packaging capacity has become a delivery bottleneck for AI chips.

Process Node Manufacturing

The semiconductor manufacturing process scale (e.g., 5nm, 3nm), affecting transistor density, efficiency, and cost. Leading AI GPUs/CPUs depend on leading nodes from foundries such as TSMC.

High Bandwidth Memory HBM Memory

Ultra-high-bandwidth DRAM via 3D stacking and through-silicon vias (TSVs), mounted beside AI accelerators. Training and large-model inference are sensitive to both capacity and bandwidth; HBM is among the scarcest components.

DRAM Dynamic Random-Access Memory Memory

Volatile memory that needs refresh; server main memory (DDR) and HBM both belong to the DRAM family. AI servers typically ship large DDR configurations for CPU-side data pipelines and multi-tenancy.

SSD Solid-State Drive Storage

Storage based on NAND flash. In training used for checkpoints, dataset caches, and feature vectors; enterprise NVMe SSDs demand high bandwidth and endurance.

HDD Hard Disk Drive Storage

Magnetic-disk storage with large capacity at low cost. Still widely used for cold data, archives, and massive raw corpora—far cheaper than SSD.

NAND Flash Storage

The medium behind SSDs and some embedded storage. SLC/MLC/TLC/QLC trade capacity against endurance. AI data lakes raise demand for high-capacity NAND.

CXL Compute Express Link Interconnect

A PCIe-based high-speed interconnect standard supporting memory pooling and cache-coherent expansion. Aims to ease the server memory wall so CPUs/accelerators can share larger memory pools.

VRAM / Device Memory Memory

Local high-speed memory on the accelerator (often HBM on AI GPUs). Holds weights, activations, optimizer state, and KV Cache. Insufficient capacity forces model parallelism or offload and sharply slows work.

KV Cache Inference

During autoregressive decoding, caches already-computed Keys/Values to avoid recomputation. At long context and large batch, KV Cache can dominate device memory—a core systems problem for inference.

Memory Bandwidth Wall Bottleneck

When compute grows faster than memory bandwidth, peak FLOPS cannot be fully utilized. LLM inference (especially decode) is often memory-bound, which is why HBM bandwidth matters so much.

HBM Generations HBM3 / HBM3E / HBM4 Memory

HBM technology iterations: more stack layers and higher per-stack bandwidth. HBM3E is the mainstream fit for 2024–2025 flagship AI GPUs; HBM4 points to higher bandwidth and more advanced interfaces.

Data Center Infrastructure

A facility that hosts compute, storage, and networking equipment at scale. AI data centers differ markedly from traditional internet DCs in power density, cooling, and network topology.

Hyperscaler Players

Cloud providers known for building and operating ultra-large data centers (e.g., AWS, Azure, Google Cloud, and major regional clouds). They are primary buyers and operators of AI compute.

Liquid Cooling Thermal

Using liquid coolants to remove server heat—cold plate, immersion, and related designs. High-power GPU clusters are migrating from air cooling toward liquid cooling.

Optical Module / Transceiver Optics

Pluggable modules that convert between electrical and optical signals (e.g., 400G/800G/1.6T). AI clusters generate huge east–west traffic, sharply raising demand for high-speed optics.

Co-Packaged Optics CPO Optics

Packaging optical engines with switch ASICs to shorten electrical paths and cut power and latency. Seen as important for next-generation high-bandwidth switching; still industrializing.

InfiniBand IB Network

Low-latency, high-bandwidth HPC networking, dominated by the NVIDIA (Mellanox) ecosystem. Widely used for GPU-cluster training fabrics (e.g., NDR 400G).

Ethernet Network

The mainstream data-center network protocol. With RoCE, Ultra Ethernet Consortium (UEC) enhancements, and similar work, it competes with IB in AI clusters. Switch ASICs (Broadcom and others) are critical.

Rack Power Density Power

Power available per rack (kW). Traditional racks may be a few to low-teens kW; AI racks can reach 40–120+ kW, forcing redesign of power distribution and cooling.

PUE Power Usage Effectiveness Efficiency

Data-center total energy divided by IT equipment energy. Closer to 1.0 is better. As AI load rises, cooling and power-distribution efficiency weigh more heavily on total cost of ownership (TCO).

NVLink / NVSwitch Interconnect

NVIDIA’s high-speed GPU-to-GPU interconnect and switching, making multi-GPU setups closer to a unified memory domain—critical for tensor and expert parallelism efficiency on large models.

Training Framework Software

Examples: PyTorch, JAX, TensorFlow. Provide autodiff, distributed-training abstractions, and ecosystem tools. PyTorch dominates research and industrial LLM training.

Inference Engine Software

Runtimes optimized for deployment—vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, TGI, llama.cpp, and others—focused on batching, paged attention, quantization, and kernel fusion.

MLOps Engineering

Engineering practices for ML systems: experiment tracking, data versioning, model registries, CI/CD, monitoring, and rollback—turning research scripts into maintainable production systems.

Vector Database Data

Databases that store and retrieve high-dimensional embedding vectors (Milvus, Pinecone, Weaviate, pgvector, and others)—infrastructure for RAG.

Orchestration / Workflow System

Organizing multi-step LLM calls, tools, and human nodes into processes. LangGraph, Temporal, and various agent frameworks live here. Reliability and observability are hard parts.

Model Parallelism Distributed

Splitting a single model across devices: tensor parallel (TP), pipeline parallel (PP), expert parallel (EP), and so on. Combined with data parallel (DP) they form 3D/4D strategies.

Checkpoint Training

Snapshots of model and optimizer state during training for fault recovery and versioning. Ultra-large model checkpoints can reach terabytes and stress storage throughput.

Embedding Representation

Mapping discrete objects (words, sentences, images) into continuous vector spaces where semantically similar items are closer. Retrieval, clustering, and recommendations depend on high-quality embeddings.

Prompt Engineering Application

Designing inputs to steer model behavior. For closed API models it is a primary adaptation method; combining it with fine-tuning and tool use is usually more robust.

On-device / Edge AI Deployment

Running models locally on phones, PCs, cars, robots, and similar devices. Pros: privacy, low latency, offline use. Constraints: compute, memory, power, and thermal limits.

Embodied AI Frontier

Giving AI perception and action in the physical world—robotics, autonomous driving, and related fields. Needs multimodal sensing, planning, control, and simulation data loops.

Synthetic Data Data

Training data generated by models or simulators to fill scarce scenarios and reduce privacy risk. Quality control and distribution shift are core challenges.

A practical habit

When you hear “buy compute,” “deploy a large model,” or “capacity is short,” ask first: which layer do they mean? A component (one HBM stack), a system (an eight-GPU server), a cluster (a 10k-GPU training pool), or an API service (billed per token)? Clear layers cut a lot of later argument.

3. AI Industry Chain Panorama

Treating AI as “just software sales” is a fast way to get lost. A more durable cut is the familiar upstream–midstream–downstream split: upstream answers “can it even run?” (silicon, power, network, data); midstream answers “can it become callable capability?” (models and platforms); downstream answers “can it embed into real work?” (products and delivery).

The three layers make money differently and on different clocks. Upstream lives on manufacturing cycles and capex; midstream on research density and engineering platforms; downstream on domain understanding and distribution. When you read industry news, first place the story on a layer—conclusions drift less that way.

Up

Upstream — Infrastructure & Hardware

Silicon, memory, interconnect, servers, data centers, data pipelines

Upstream rarely trends on social feeds, yet it sets the ceiling for everything that follows. Without advanced process nodes and HBM, elegant model papers never land on silicon; without power and cooling, racks are expensive space heaters; without high-speed interconnect, 10k-GPU clusters spend much of their life waiting on each other. Reading upstream is reading constraints—not slogans.

Accelerators & Processors

For training and general inference, the default answer is still the GPU: flexible, mature software stack, able to follow model-structure shifts. When workloads are stable and large enough, ASICs / TPUs often look better on unit cost—which is why hyperscalers keep building custom silicon. The CPU still does scheduling and data plumbing; the FPGA is more a specialized scalpel than a main hammer.

TypeStrengthsTrade-offsTypical use
GPU Full ecosystem, flexible, fast iteration High unit price, lead times, high power Training, general inference
TPU / training ASIC Scale efficiency, deep internal-stack optimization Closed ecosystem, less general Hyperscale training
Inference ASIC / NPU Unit inference cost, power advantage Model porting cost, toolchain High-QPS inference, edge
CPU General-purpose, compatibility, complex control Matrix throughput far below accelerators Scheduling, prep, small models

The chip value chain also includes EDA tools (design), IP cores, wafer foundry, and advanced packaging & test. A flagship AI GPU from design to volume involves deep global specialization; any link (e.g., CoWoS packaging capacity) can become the delivery bottleneck.

Memory & Storage Hierarchy

Building AI systems is often less about “computing” than about “moving data.” The hierarchy is clear: device-adjacent HBM → host DDR → hot data NVMe → cold object store / HDD. Lower layers are cheaper and larger—and slower. Training hammers both HBM and parallel filesystems; inference decode often stalls on HBM bandwidth—the chip can compute, but data cannot keep up.

  • HBM: scarce in both capacity and bandwidth, co-packaged with logic, highly concentrated suppliers.
  • Server DRAM: multi-socket CPUs plus large memory configs push DDR5; CXL memory pooling is early but directionally clear.
  • SSD / NAND: checkpoints, data loading, and feature caches quietly consume lots of enterprise drives.
  • HDD: still the best economics for raw corpora and cold archives—not everything needs flash.
Where storage pressure comes from

People often see one weight file and forget the training floor: multiple data replicas, frequent checkpoints, experiment branches, logs, and eval sets; after launch, multi-version models, LoRAs, and vector indexes. The state lifecycle is often orders of magnitude larger than “one .safetensors.”

Networking Fabric

The stronger each card is, the more scale depends on “how cards talk.” Inside the box, NVLink-class links; between boxes, InfiniBand or enhanced Ethernet. Training AllReduce, MoE AllToAll, and pipeline bubbles all turn network problems into effective-compute (MFU) problems: when the fabric stalls, GPUs idle.

  • Optical modules: 400G → 800G → 1.6T is a clear ramp; AI clusters are port-dense, so volume and ASP often rise together.
  • Switches: leaf–spine remains the mainstream skeleton; Broadcom and NVIDIA networking are common options.
  • IB vs Ethernet: IB is mature on latency and congestion control; enhanced Ethernet often wins on unified ops and controllable cost.
  • CPO / LPO: directions for next-gen power and density; still climbing the industrialization curve—do not treat them as fully landed everywhere.

Servers & Systems

Buying chips is only half the story. Systems integration must pack accelerators, CPUs, memory, local storage, NICs, power, and cooling into deliverable, maintainable boxes— often 8-GPU trainers, 4-GPU inference servers, or liquid-cooled whole racks. OEMs, ODM/white-box, and cloud custom machines coexist with different cadences and service models.

The hard parts are usually engineering details: power redundancy, topology and cabling, firmware security, remote maintenance. One sentence worth repeating: having chips is not the same as having a production-ready cluster.

Data Center Infrastructure

An AI hall is first a power and thermodynamics problem, and only second a count of GPUs. As rack power density rises, distribution, cooling, and site selection are rewritten together— electricity price, grid-connection windows, climate, and policy all enter the project plan.

  • Power: high-voltage feed, substations, UPS/HVDC, backup generation; many projects queue on grid interconnect, not budget.
  • Cooling: once air hits its limit, cold-plate liquid cooling, immersion, and rear-door heat exchangers become real options.
  • Facility: floor load, cable trays, leak detection, fire safety, and physical security—dull, but decisive for sustained full load.

Data Pipeline

Data is the invisible upstream. What a model can and cannot do is still bounded by corpus quality, domain coverage, and feedback data. The chain runs from collection and licensing through cleaning, dedup, scoring, synthesis, labeling, and compliance—every step is labor-intensive.

Pre-training looks more like an industrial pipeline; alignment and domain adaptation lean harder on expert data and process design. After launch, whether the “use–feedback–retrain” loop actually spins often decides gaps more than buying another batch of cards.

Mid

Midstream — Models & Platforms

Foundation models, frameworks, cloud platforms, tooling

Midstream in one sentence: turn electricity and silicon into capability others will call. The moats are not mysterious—research judgment, data and experiment systems, large-scale training engineering, stable inference products, and whether developers will follow you. Commercially that can mean closed APIs, open weights plus services, or cloud rental and private deployment—different paths, different constraints.

Closed frontier models are usually iterated by a few labs/companies, competing on capability boundaries, safety operations, and product finish. Open-weight routes lower the bar: local deploy, secondary development, and vertical adaptation get easier. The paths are not mutually exclusive; they serve different constraints.

Real selection rarely asks only “who is #1.” The usual trade-offs are reasoning and code, multimodality, long context, tool use, latency, stability, and cost per million tokens. A single “strongest” almost never exists; scenario fit and TCO are often more honest than leaderboards.

  • Pre-training: roughly sets world knowledge and the capability ceiling
  • Post-training: decides how well it follows, style, and whether it can think step by step
  • Systems layer: tools, retrieval, memory—whether the product is usable every day

On the training side, PyTorch remains the common language for most teams, plus distributed combinations like FSDP / Megatron / DeepSpeed; compilation and graph optimization slowly turn “it runs” into “it runs thriftily.” Inference is a throughput race: engines like vLLM live on paged attention and continuous batching; TensorRT-LLM chases extreme kernels; the edge has a lighter toolchain of its own.

A plain truth: good software is free compute. Same cards, lower throughput, and the bill reflects it honestly.

Cloud packages “buy cards, build halls, staff ops” into rentable form: instances, managed training, model APIs, retrieval, and compliance networks. Hyperscalers are simultaneously the largest upstream buyers, midstream platform providers, and sometimes custom-chip and model builders—vertical integration is an account structure, not a slogan.

For enterprise selection, price is only one item. Region, quota, private-link bandwidth, data residency, SLA, and bill shape often become real pain points earlier.

Demos can run on scripts; production needs systems: experiment and data versions, evaluation, prompt and app versioning, observability for latency/cost/quality, plus permissions and guardrails. Vector stores support retrieval-augmented generation; orchestration frameworks support multi-step tool use. Without them, even a strong model is only “occasional surprise.”

This layer has many tools that churn quickly. Architect for replaceability rather than betting on a single “full suite.”

Down

Downstream — Applications & Delivery

Consumer apps, enterprise verticals, edge AI, agents & embodied systems

Downstream answers the last question: do money and liability close the loop? Few successful products simply “expose a bare model.” More often you see workflow embedding, permissions and data wiring, evaluation and humans-in-the-loop, controllable cost, and explainable compliance. The model is the engine; delivery is the vehicle.

Consumer Applications

  • General assistants / search augmentation: dialogue, Q&A, summarization, planning
  • Content creation: copy, image, video, music, design assist
  • Productivity tools: office suites, email, notes, coding assistants
  • Education & entertainment: personalized tutoring, NPCs, interactive narrative

Monetization can be subscription, ads, in-app purchase, or device preinstall. The real survival metrics are plainer: do people return, can tasks complete, how often do failures happen, and can per-active-user inference cost be covered.

On-device AI

Phone NPUs, local PC small models, cabin and ADAS domain controllers, XR devices—all pull some inference back on-device. A sensible split is usually: small jobs local, heavy jobs in the cloud. Quantization, distillation, speculative decoding, heterogeneous scheduling, and private local indexes are engineering tools for that split.

Agents & Embodied AI

Agents try to turn “answer once” into “finish multi-step work.” Demos are easy; stability is hard: reliability, cost, and safety boundaries remain tough. Embodied AI goes further, requiring perception and action in the physical world—simulation, closed-loop control, and real-world evaluation are all required. Both lines remind us: AI is growing back from pure software into systems engineering.

4. How Large Models Are Trained

External narratives often reduce training to “pour in data, wait for the model to get stronger.” Internally it is a long pipeline: data must be cleaned, sequences packed, pre-training burns accelerators, alignment involves people, and release still needs safety and evaluation gates. Below we break stages and name what each truly consumes—hardware, software, people, data, or power. Names vary slightly across labs; order and overlap are common—do not memorize labels as dogma.

4.2 Stage-by-Stage

  1. Data Collection

    From crawls and licensed content, code and books, to multimodal and synthetic data. Legal and copyright issues are not an appendix—they are a starting condition.

    Hardware: crawl clusters, object storage, bandwidth Software: ingestion pipelines, dedup indexes People: data engineering, legal Power: moderate
  2. Cleaning & Filtering

    Quality scoring, detox, dedup, PII handling, domain mix. This stage is boring, yet decides whether later power burn is worth it. Garbage in, garbage out.

    Small-scale CPU/GPU filter inference Data lakehouse, feature stores Data science + label QA sampling
  3. Tokenization & Packing

    Train or choose a tokenizer, pack documents into training sequences, set curriculum and mixture ratios. Affects training efficiency and model behavior.

    Mostly CPU, high disk throughput Output: tokenized binary data
  4. Pre-training

    The real money-burn main stage. Multi-thousand-GPU clusters for weeks to months: fault tolerance, recomputation, mixed precision, parallelism strategy—any weak link lengthens the bill.

    Massive GPU + HBM + IB/Ethernet Frequent TB-scale checkpoints Research scientists + training eng + SRE Very high sustained power
  5. Mid-training / Annealing

    Continue training on higher-quality data or special mixes, or lower learning rate to stabilize. Used to lift capability and stability.

    Still needs large-scale accelerators Denser evaluation loops
  6. Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

    Instruction–response pairs, tool-use traces, domain task demos. Data quality density is far higher than pre-training.

    Small-to-medium GPU Domain experts and labeling
  7. Preference Alignment

    RLHF (reward model + RL), DPO, and similar methods. Optimize for “more helpful, harmless, honest,” and related goals; hyperparameters are sensitive.

    Medium GPU + sampling compute Preference labeling, red team
  8. Safety, Eval, Release

    Capability benchmarks, adversarial tests, regression evals, licensing and release decisions. Open-weight and API product release processes differ.

  9. Inference Serving

    Quantization, batching, autoscaling, multi-region, observability, and cost attribution. Most of the value users touch is delivered at this layer.

    Inference GPU/ASIC fleets Power that tracks QPS

4.3 Why So Much HBM?

What memory holds during training

Not only weights. Also gradients, optimizer state (Adam-class optimizers can expand state to multiples of parameter count), activations (or recompute to trade space), and communication buffers. Larger models, longer sequences, and bigger batches quickly overflow a single card, forcing split parallelism; after the split, each card still needs HBM that is large and fast enough, or Tensor Cores starve. HBM is expensive because it sells both capacity and bandwidth at once.

At inference, the bottleneck is often “move,” not “math”

Weights must live nearby (or be tier-offloaded); even thornier is the KV Cache, which grows linearly with batch and sequence length. Decode is often memory-bound: arithmetic units idle while the bus thrash-moves data. Then HBM bandwidth directly sets latency and throughput.

4.4 Why Storage Is Consumed So Aggressively

  • Raw data lakes and multiple cleaned versions coexist for a long time
  • Checkpoint frequency × replicas × experiment branches grows volume fast
  • Eval sets, generated samples, logs, and metrics must be traceable
  • Online multi-version models, LoRAs, caches, and vector indexes

So beside almost every AI cluster sits a non-cheap parallel filesystem and object store. The GPU purchase order is only one page of the bill.

4.5 Why Power Became a Hard Constraint

A single card can draw hundreds of watts or more; densify racks and networking, storage, and cooling stack on top. The limit is often not “electricity is a bit expensive,” but whether the substation has capacity, how long grid interconnect takes, whether land and water suffice, and whether policy allows. Site selection moves toward power-rich regions; long-term PPAs, nuclear, and renewables enter the conversation; chips and algorithms seriously optimize tokens per watt.

A more accurate sentence

“Compute shortage” rarely means “the world lacks a part called GPU.” More accurately: within a time window, system capacity of advanced accelerators + HBM + packaging + networking + power and facility delivery is insufficient. Unblock one link and the bottleneck politely moves to the next.

5. Enabling Supply Chains

Model launch stages put the spotlight on algorithms and products; the supply chain quietly decides “can we ship on time.” The lines below deserve a separate look: they never appear in the chat window, yet decide whether there is power, cards, and light behind it.

Logic chips depend on EUV and advanced-node yield; AI accelerators add another critical step—advanced packaging (e.g., CoWoS-class) that tightly joins SoC and multi-stack HBM. In recent years packaging and test capacity have repeatedly been a more real delivery bottleneck than “design complete.” Further upstream sit equipment makers such as ASML, Applied Materials, Lam, and Tokyo Electron.

  • Design: architecture, circuits, physical design, verification
  • Manufacture: wafers, masks, yield ramp
  • Package: interposers, bumps, stacking, test
  • System: board, full system, cluster validation

Why expensive:

  • 3D stacking and TSV processes are complex; yield challenges exceed commodity DRAM
  • Co-packaged with logic, high test and matching cost
  • Demand highly concentrated on flagship AI accelerators; long-term contracts lock supply
  • R&D and line conversion require large capital expenditure

Why tight:

  • Limited suppliers (SK Hynix, Samsung, Micron, and peers)
  • Long expansion cycles—cannot double elastically by quarter
  • Competes with consumer DRAM/NAND for some manufacturing resources and attention
  • Each HBM generation tightly binds to new GPU launches; ramp windows overlap

Grid interconnect, substations, backup power, and long-term power pricing are already a parallel mainline of AI capex. “Have land and money, no power” is not a joke—it is a real status on project plans. Tokens per watt shifts from slogan to design metric.

From DSPs and lasers to fiber, connectors, modules, and switches, optical communications is a full chain. Training clusters drive huge east–west traffic; 800G / 1.6T demand ramps fast. Pluggable modules remain the main act; CPO is still in pilots and standards evolution—worth following, not yet “already fully replaced.”

Cold plate, immersion, CDUs, cooling towers, leak prevention, and maintenance procedures—options exist, and cost is more than the equipment list. Cooling changes rack layout, power design, ops skills, even the whole TCO sheet. Treating it as “add more fans” is a tuition bill paid on site.

Licensed content, labeling, synthetic data, privacy-preserving compute, regional data-flow arrangements, and internal enterprise governance together form the “invisible upstream.” High-quality public web text is thinning as a free ride; proprietary data, interaction logs, and real-world multimodal data increasingly look like moats.

7. Myths & Calibrated Takes

These lines show up often at dinners and on social media. Not to argue—only to recalibrate judgment back to ground you can test.

Myth 1: “More parameters always means stronger”

Parameters are one chapter. Data quality, training recipe, post-training, test-time compute, and tool use all change endings. A smaller, well-trained model with good strategy and systems can beat a larger under-trained rival on specific tasks.

Better framing

With similar recipes, scale often helps; comparing parameter count across recipes alone means little.

Myth 2: “AI is only a software problem—buy enough cards”

HBM, packaging, networking, power, cooling, software efficiency, and people can all bottleneck. Cards sitting in warehouses, or racked but underutilized, are not rare stories.

Better framing

Compute is necessary, never sufficient. This is systems engineering.

Myth 3: “Once open source ships, closed models lose all value”

Open source lowers barriers and speeds diffusion; closed models can still close the loop on frontier capability, product experience, safety operations, and continuous iteration. They serve different budgets and risk appetites.

Better framing

Parallel ecosystems, not zero-sum extinction.

Myth 4: “Add RAG and hallucinations disappear”

RAG reduces freestyle invention. Wrong retrieval, dirty context, models that ignore evidence, or stale indexes still produce errors. Critical scenarios still need citations, verification, and permissions.

Better framing

RAG is a seatbelt, not a perpetual-motion machine.

Myth 5: “Agents will fully replace employees soon”

Agents excel at tool-able, verifiable sub-tasks. Liability, exception handling, organizational coordination, and messy reality still need people in volume. More likely is redivision of labor, not one-click empty desks.

Better framing

Watch task automation rates and supervision cost—more useful than replacement narratives.

Myth 6: “Chip efficiency doubles every year, so power problems vanish”

Efficiency is improving—that is real. But longer context, more users, more modalities, heavier test-time compute—demand rises too. Grids and halls are built in years, not quarterly release notes.

Better framing

Efficiency and demand are racing; infrastructure needs multi-year planning.

Myth 7: “#1 on the board = ready for production”

Public boards can be polluted, overfit, or mismatched to your scenario. Production cares about business metrics, latency, cost, stability, safety, and operability.

Better framing

Private eval sets and online A/B beat chasing leaderboards.

8. FAQ for Readers

These questions come from the batch almost everyone asks when first seriously unpacking the industry. Answers do not claim to be “the official answer”—only to help you ask the right next question. Being able to keep thinking beats memorizing slogans.

Industry structure

Why can’t we treat the AI industry as “just selling software”?

Because software sits only on the top layer. Below sit silicon, packaging, HBM, optics, halls, power, and data. No matter how strong the model, it must land on deliverable compute and affordable inference cost. Viewing AI as pure software systematically underestimates delivery cycles and capital intensity.

Where do profit pools tend to sit across upstream, midstream, and downstream?

There is no eternal answer, but a common pattern: scarce and hard-to-copy links price better. In recent cycles, advanced accelerators, HBM, advanced packaging, and parts of optical communications held harder margins and bargaining power; midstream needs differentiated models and platform stickiness; downstream needs scenarios and distribution. Profits follow bottlenecks—where the bottleneck moves, money often follows.

When reading AI news, how do I quickly map a headline to the stack?

Filter with four words: component (HBM/GPU), system (server), cluster (10k GPUs / power), service (API/app). A headline about “compute shortage” may mean cards—or packaging or power. Pin the layer first, then debate ups and downs; half the argument disappears.

Clouds buy cards, train models, and sell APIs—is that “referee and player”?

A more accurate phrase is vertical integration: largest upstream buyer, midstream platform, and downstream app entry can stack in one firm. That does not automatically equal unfairness, but it means choosing a vendor is also choosing an ecosystem and bargaining structure. Focus on portability, quotas, price curves, and lock-in cost—not moral storytelling.

Compute, HBM, power

Why doesn’t “GPU arrived” mean “ready to train”?

Cards are parts. You still need matching HBM and packaging, in-box and inter-box networking, power and cooling, hall space, scheduling software, and people who can operate it. Any lagging link can leave GPUs as fixed assets in a warehouse rather than producing tokens.

Why is HBM expensive and tight—is it just hype?

Not entirely. HBM process (stacking, TSV, co-package with logic) is hard; yield and test cost are high; suppliers are concentrated; expansion is measured in years. Demand is tightly bound to flagship accelerators. Price reflects difficulty and matching; tightness reflects expansion lagging order windows. Prices fluctuate, but “hard” is not fiction.

Why is inference often “bandwidth-bound” rather than “FLOPS-bound”?

Generating the next token involves repeatedly moving weights and KV Cache. Arithmetic units may idle while the bus is busy—that is memory-bound. So at long context, large batch, and high concurrency, HBM bandwidth and caching strategy often decide felt latency more than peak FLOPS.

Will power stay a long-term bottleneck if chips get more efficient?

Efficiency is improving, but demand also rises: longer context, more users, more modalities, test-time compute. Both sides race. Grids and halls are built in years; interconnect windows cannot “cloud-scale.” Power is a multi-year constraint—not erased by “more efficient next year.”

How do training and inference differ in resource profile?

Training is a long campaign: communication-heavy, fault-tolerant, checkpointed, high sustained power—goal is to finish the data in acceptable time. Inference is a shop open day and night: latency, throughput, cost, multi-tenancy, and version management. The same card can serve both, but cluster design and software stacks usually need separate thinking.

Models, open source, products

Should I pick open-weight models or closed APIs?

Follow constraints, not camps. Want strongest general capability with less ops? Closed APIs often save care. Need private data, customization, and a controllable cost curve? Open weights fit better. Many serious teams mix: one path as primary, another as edge and backup. The real questions are TCO, compliance, and exit ramps when things fail.

Parameters, leaderboards, or demos—what deserves trust?

Alone, none are enough. Parameters hint at capacity, not proof of ability; boards can be overfit; demos can be cherry-picked. Production trusts your own task set, latency and cost curves, online incident rates, and whether versions can roll back.

Does RAG “fix” hallucination? When is it not enough?

RAG reduces confident invention about the unknown world—if retrieval is right, context is clean, and the model follows evidence. Wrong retrieval, stale indexes, messy permissions, or models that ignore citations still produce errors. Critical work needs citations, checks, and human gates—a seatbelt is not a perpetual-motion machine.

When is an agent worth building, and when is it just noise?

When tasks can be stepped, tools can be called, results verified, and failures rolled back, agents can show real ROI. When liability is high, exceptions are many, environments are messy, and supervision costs more than humans, forcing an agent hides complexity in a black box. First price “supervision cost + failure cost,” then talk autonomy level.

Will on-device AI replace cloud large models?

More likely a division of labor: small jobs local (privacy, low latency, offline), heavy jobs in the cloud (large models, multimodality, peak compute). On-device is bound by power and memory; cloud by cost and network. Asking “who replaces whom” is less useful than “which class of request should stop at which layer.”

Business & organization

Is “compute is power” true?

At the training frontier and large-scale inference, compute is close to hard currency. It is not the only currency: data, algorithmic efficiency, product distribution, and brand trust can also rewrite outcomes. Small teams with better data and systems can sometimes win specific battles against larger clusters. Compute is a necessary constraint, not the whole story.

What if inference gets 10× cheaper?

Many “demo-grade” features become default switches: longer context, more diligent agents, finer personalization, more silent background calls. Usage may rebound (Jevons-paradox style), and power plus product-abuse risks can rise together. Cost drops release demand; they do not automatically dissolve problems.

Where do enterprise projects most often die?

Rarely only on “the model is not smart enough.” More common: data permissions cannot connect, workflows will not embed, evaluation is unclear, cost is uncontrollable, compliance fails, nobody owns outcomes. Tech selection matters; organization and process are often more fatal.

What is realistic for individuals or small teams in this chain?

You need not start by building chips or training trillion-parameter models. More realistic cuts: vertical data, workflow products, evaluation and safety, inference optimization, industry integration, developer tools. The chain is long; profit is not only under the spotlight. Find a narrow scene you understand better than giants—usually more rational than frontal assault on foundation models.

How do I judge whether an AI industry story is overheated?

Check four things: can evaluation be reproduced, is unit cost disclosed, is capacity/delivery verifiable, and are failure modes mentioned. Vision without constraints is usually marketing. Steep progress exists, but rarely arrives as magic.

Learning & judgment

Without a hardware background, do I still need HBM and liquid cooling?

You need not draw packaging cross-sections, but you should know how they become latency, cost, and delivery time. Product managers who do not understand “why expensive, why slow” easily promise customers things physics will not allow. Knowing constraints is part of professionalism.

Information moves fast—will this guide go stale quickly?

Specific SKUs and prices will age; layer structure and constraint logic move much more slowly. Upstream–midstream–downstream division of labor, training vs inference resource profiles, and the mechanisms of HBM/power/network as bottlenecks last longer than any season’s flagship name. Treat this page as a map; treat news as weather.

After reading, what is a good next step?

Pick a product or project you actually care about and draw a half-page dependency map: model, inference cost, data, compliance, and the physical constraint most likely to block you. If you can draw it clearly, coordinates are standing; unclear spots are next week’s reading list.

Beyond Q&A

Good questions are often scarcer than good answers. If you keep only one line after reading, let it be: First see which layer you stand on and which constraints bind you—then decide which narrative to trust.

9. Further Reading & Learning Paths

You need not read every material in the world in order. Pick the path closest to your goal and let a real question drag you forward—that is usually most efficient.

Path A · Build industry coordinates first

For: people who want to understand news and constraints

  • Read this page’s panorama, glossary, and “Myths” thin and solid
  • Track cloud capex, GPU roadmaps, hall and power news—and learn to separate “marketing” from “delivery”
  • Pick one public industry report or semiconductor/optics survey to practice finding the main thread
  • Exercise: take an AI product you use and reverse its model, cost, data, and compliance dependencies
Path B · Training & inference engineering

For: people who need to run systems stably

  • Fill foundations: linear-algebra intuition, probability, deep-learning basics, Python
  • Frameworks: PyTorch tutorials → build a small Transformer yourself
  • Systems: mixed precision, FSDP/ZeRO, communication primitives, where MFU comes from
  • Inference: KV Cache, quantization, engines like vLLM, performance profiling
  • Data: cleaning, eval design, what SFT/preference alignment actually do
Path C · Hardware & infrastructure

For: people who want to understand cards, nets, power, halls

  • Architecture: memory hierarchy, parallelism, interconnect
  • Public GPU architecture materials and a CUDA programming-model overview
  • HBM, packaging, optical modules: get the layers right before industry detail
  • Data centers: PUE, liquid cooling, leaf–spine networks—treat the hall as a product
Path D · Product & delivery

For: people who must turn models into reliable services

  • RAG: chunking, retrieval, rerank, citations, evaluation
  • Agents: tool calls, state, permissions, human review nodes
  • Quality: offline sets + online monitoring + red team—not vibes alone
  • Cost: caching, large/small model routing, batching, edge–cloud split
A filter for industry news

When words like “disrupted,” “overnight,” or “total lead” appear, pause one second and ask four things: Can evaluation be reproduced? What is unit cost? Can capacity deliver? Are energy and failure cases mentioned? Real progress is often steep, but rarely magic.
