Research proposal prepared for discussion with Professor Baizhu Chen

Spreads Are Not Probabilities
Delivery-Adjusted U.S. Sovereign Default Risk

Research collaboration proposal · 31 August 2026

Commentary treats one-year U.S. sovereign CDS spreads as a thermometer of default risk. They are not. A CDS premium prices the product of a risk-adjusted default probability and a loss given default, and for the United States the loss is dominated by the cheapest-to-deliver Treasury rather than by a recovery convention. Across four debt-limit episodes the two objects disagree. In May 2023 the one-year spread reached 177 basis points — two to three times the 2011 and 2013 peaks — while a delivery-adjusted implied default probability of about 4 percent was below 2011 (6 percent) and similar to 2013. In 2025 the same probability peaked near 1 percent, even as five-year U.S. CDS traded through several European sovereigns. The proposed project rebuilds that measurement from public sources, places it in a pre-specified four-instrument stack (CDS, bills around the x-date, cash–futures, and swap spreads), and uses the August 2026 high-real-rate, ordinary-slope state as a non-event contrast: fiscal duration without a credit event. The contribution is measurement, not a political theory of the debt limit.

Research question and proposed contribution

When U.S. sovereign CDS widen around a fiscal deadline, how much of the move is credit repricing, how much is cheapest-to-deliver mechanics, and when is the same fiscal news showing up as duration or convenience rather than as default?

The question is well-posed because the United States is the only large sovereign for which the cheapest deliverable into a CDS auction is a deep, regularly issued government bond whose price can move for purely monetary-policy reasons. A 2020 1.25 percent bond due May 2050 traded near 58 in early May 2023, not because the Treasury was insolvent, but because the policy rate had risen. That cheapening raises the CDS payout in a technical default and therefore raises the spread even if the probability of the credit event is unchanged. Treating the spread as a probability therefore mixes a political object with an interest-rate put.

Economic content

Debt-limit episodes are discrete political shocks. They should, if they are about default, invert the CDS curve, kink bills that mature near the x-date, and leave the long real curve comparatively untouched. If they are about fiscal duration or convenience, the opposite pattern should appear: a high long real yield, an ordinary slope, and a quiet one-year default probability. August 2026 is already measured on the curve side. The missing half is a comparable default-probability object.

Measurement content

The project documents a public, episode-consistent pipeline: identify the cheapest deliverable each day from the outstanding coupon universe, convert one-year CDS into a risk-adjusted default probability under both a constant-hazard convention and an x-date-timed convention, and test whether bills, futures, and swap spreads agree. Thin trading is reported as a result, not hidden in a footnote.

Relation to work already on the table. A public 2023 note (Sparks and Wang, MSCI) showed that the May 3, 2023 implied one-year default probability was 3.9 percent — lower than 2011 despite a much wider spread — once the cheapest-to-deliver price was used as recovery. Benzoni, Cabanilla, Cocco and Kavoussi (Chicago Fed, 2023, 2025) independently reach the same qualitative ranking with a timed-default formula. That ranking is the starting fact, not the paper. What remains is a replicable public pipeline, a pre-specified cross-instrument protocol, a 2025–26 update, and a non-event contrast that the curve work has already measured.

Why the ranking reverses

Write \(s\) for the one-year running spread, \(P^{\mathrm{CTD}}\) for the price of the cheapest deliverable per 100 of face, and \(L = 1 - P^{\mathrm{CTD}}/100\) for the implied loss given a technical default that does not further cheapen the bond. A first-order map is

\[ s \approx p\cdot L, \qquad p \approx \frac{s}{1-P^{\mathrm{CTD}}/100}. \tag{1} \]

Constant-hazard, zero-rate, one-period illustration. The working specification below replaces this with an x-date-timed survival curve.

In 2011 the cheapest deliverable still traded near par, so \(L\) was small and a spread of roughly 80 basis points mapped into a 6 percent probability. In 2023 the 1.25s of May 2050 — issued near par in the 2020 rate trough — traded near 55–58. The same political object (a possible missed payment) now paid 42–45 points rather than a handful of points. Investors paid a wider spread for a smaller implied \(p\). That is the sense in which U.S. sovereign CDS is a hybrid of credit and an interest-rate put (Sparks and Wang 2023).

Price of the 1.25s of 15 May 2050, the bond Chicago Fed and MSCI identify as the 2023 cheapest deliverable. Published marks: about 49.4 on 21 October 2022 (episode low), about 55 in spring 2023, about 58 on 3 May 2023. Outstanding face on that CUSIP exceeded $73 billion against $5.7 billion of net U.S. sovereign CDS notional in the week of 12 May 2023, so an auction would likely have cleared near this price (Benzoni et al. 2023). The 1.375s of 15 August 2050 were only slightly richer.

A working laboratory: spread, recovery, and probability

Equation (1) is enough to see the identification. Hold the probability fixed and the spread must rise when the cheapest deliverable cheapens; hold the spread fixed and the implied probability falls. MSCI’s published comparative static is that a 5 percent decline in the cheapest-to-deliver price, with \(p\) held at 4 percent, raises the one-year spread by about 13 basis points. The sliders recompute that map. They are a teaching device, not an estimator.

The 2023 spike was, on the published attribution, almost entirely a vertical move: long-end prices were range-bound through 3 May, so the 146 bp widening from early 2023 mapped into \(p\) rather than into \(L\) (Sparks and Wang 2023).

Empirical design

The estimator has to do three things the one-period map does not: put the credit event on the political calendar, discount on a curve that is not itself the Treasury par curve, and refuse to treat a quote as a price when the market did not trade.

Timed default at the x-date

Debt-limit default, if it occurs, is not a constant-hazard Poisson event. It is concentrated on a window around the Treasury’s exhaustion date. Following Sundaresan (2009) as implemented by Benzoni et al. (2023), let \(s(i)\) be the risk-adjusted survival probability through quarter \(i\), \(c\) the annual premium, \(B_0(i)\) a non-Treasury discount factor, and \(L_t\) the current cheapest-to-deliver discount to par. For a one-year contract originated at \(t\),

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{4}s(i)\,B_0(i)\frac{c}{4} +\sum_{i=1}^{4}p(i)\,B_0\!\left(i-\tfrac12\right)\frac{c}{8} = L_t\sum_{i=1}^{4}p(i)\,B_0\!\left(i-\tfrac12\right), \tag{2} \]

with \(p(i)=s(i-1)-s(i)\). The identifying restriction for an episode is that \(p(i)\) is zero except in the quarter that contains the contemporaneous x-date, where it equals a single \(p_t\). That \(p_t\) is the object reported in Figure 7 of the Chicago Fed essay and the object this project will reconstruct daily. Discounting uses OIS or SOFR term rates, not CMT: during a debt-limit episode the Treasury curve is the thing being tested.

Two numbers, not one. The paper will always report both the timed \(p_t\) from (2) and the constant-hazard map (1). The 2011–2023 ranking is robust to that choice in the published work; the 2025–26 comparison may not be, because the x-date window and the cheapest deliverable both move. Disagreement between (1) and (2) is a result.

Cheapest-to-deliver construction

Each day \(t\), the deliverable set is the universe of marketable coupon Treasuries with remaining maturity of at most thirty years, as specified by the ISDA physical-settlement matrix for the relevant sovereign transaction type (U.S. sovereign CDS trades on European-sovereign standard terms: failure to pay, moratorium, and restructuring). The cheapest-to-deliver price is

\[ P^{\mathrm{CTD}}_t = \min_{j\in\mathcal D_t} P_{j,t}, \qquad L_t = 1 - P^{\mathrm{CTD}}_t/100. \tag{3} \]

In 2023 the minimizer was stable — the 1.25s of May 2050, with the 1.375s of August 2050 a close second. In 2011 and 2013 the minimizer rotated, which is why Benzoni et al. average the five preceding calendar days. The pipeline will store the identity of the minimizer, not only its price, and will flag days on which the second-cheapest is more than a point away. Auction-clearing risk is treated separately: when net CDS notional is small relative to the cheapest issue’s float, \(P^{\mathrm{CTD}}_t\) is a defensible auction proxy; when it is not, the paper reports a blended-recovery band rather than a point.

The comparative claim is then simple enough to falsify. If 2025–26 is a regime of structurally wider CDS for a given \(p_t\), the rates-put term should account for it: the cheapest deliverable is still a low-coupon 2050s bond, and a 30-year par yield of 5.22 percent on 28 August 2026 keeps that bond at a deep discount. If instead \(p_t\) itself has risen while bills do not kink, the market is pricing a different object than a missed payment in a particular month — a longer-horizon fiscal default in the sense of Chernov, Schmid and Schneider (2020), not a debt-limit event. If neither \(p_t\) nor the bill kink moves, August 2026 stays on the duration side of the table, which is what the completed curve thesis already suggests.

Liquidity, quanto, and data quality as results

U.S. sovereign CDS is a small market that wakes up for political deadlines. Gross notional peaked at $32.3 billion in August 2011, fell to $1.3 billion by 2021:Q4, and reached $13.3 billion in the week of 12 May 2023. Most weeks in 2022 saw no trades; the week of 26 May 2023 saw 178 contracts, then three or four a week after the FRA. A majority of contracts are euro-denominated, so the premium embeds a quanto on dollar depreciation in default (Augustin, Chernov and Song 2020). Klingler and Lando (2018) give a further non-credit motive: dealer capital relief on uncollateralized sovereign derivatives.

The paper therefore reports, for every peak, the DTCC trade count, net notional, 1y–5y slope, and USD/EUR basis when both quotes exist. A probability estimated off three contracts is labeled as such. That is closer to Chen’s instinct about what a number can bear than a single Bloomberg series treated as a price.

Data constraint, stated in advance. Historical single-name CDS is the binding input. Candidate public or personally licensed sources: DTCC TIW aggregates, any university Markit/S&P Global access, and contemporaneous published prints (Chicago Fed, MSCI, CRS, Reuters, FT) as an audit tape. If a continuous 2011–2026 one-year series cannot be licensed, the paper is built on episode windows plus the public prints, and that limitation is in the abstract. Employer terminals are not a fallback.

Chen’s 2018 paper ranks equity markets by systemic contribution. The natural extension, already sketched as a companion project, is to put credit nodes into that system. This proposal is narrower and should be first. Without a defensible \(p_t\), a U.S. sovereign CDS spread fed into ΔCoVaR is just another noisy equity-correlated spread. With it, one can ask whether equity-market systemic rankings move when the sovereign object is a 6 percent timed default probability (2011) or a 1 percent probability sitting on a 99th-percentile real long yield (2026). That is a question a Marshall / CASS / CES audience will recognize, and it is a question that needs the measurement half before the spillover half.

Division of work, scope, and constraints

What I would build

  • Daily CTD identifier from the public coupon universe.
  • Timed and constant-hazard \(p_t\) from whatever CDS tape can be licensed or reconstructed.
  • Bill–OIS x-date kinks, futures CTD, and swap-spread overlays.
  • The August 2026 curve contrast, already completed on official par yields.
  • A replicable public archive: hashes, episode calendars, and a one-figure notebook that can be rerun when the next x-date appears.

Where Professor Chen’s judgment is the scarce input

  • Which political dates are treatment and which are noise — including 2025’s overlap of a debt limit, a tax bill, and Liberation Day tariffs.
  • How far a thin CDS print can be asked to go in a paper that will be read by policy and practitioner audiences.
  • Whether the right first paper is the four-episode measurement note or the measurement-plus-ΔCoVaR companion.
  • Placement and framing. This is not a job inquiry; it is an ask for identification and audience.
