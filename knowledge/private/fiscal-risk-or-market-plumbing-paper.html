High Real Rates, Ordinary Slopes
Structural Change and the August 2026 U.S. Treasury Market

Thesis
30 August 2026

Abstract. Long-dated U.S. Treasury yields reached unusual post-2010 levels in August 2026, inviting explanations based on fiscal-duration risk, common macroeconomic forces, and market functioning. Using 2,916 daily official nominal and real par-yield curves from 2 January 2015–28 August 2026, this paper develops a nested diagnostic design that separates levels, residual curve shape, innovations, policy-conditioned residuals, and a local announcement window. Nominal and real 10- and 30-year yields exceed the 99th percentiles of the matched 2015–26 sample, while 30y–2y and 30y–10y slopes remain near their medians; approximate par breakevens lie only between the 67th and 75th percentiles. A joint standardized PCA assigns 50.21% of first-component loading energy to nominal yields and 49.79% to real yields, confirming that the dominant state is genuinely shared. Although capped piecewise-mean screens produce aligned level boundaries (p = 0.0211 in a conditional date-randomization benchmark), neither nominal nor real policy-conditioned residuals select a boundary after removing factor persistence and effective-federal-funds-rate dynamics. The 18–19 August long-short rotation reaches the 97.4th percentile (p = 0.0309), but disappears over three- and five-day windows. The evidence therefore supports a high-real-rate, ordinary-slope macro state with a transitory localized response—not separate identification of fiscal risk or market plumbing.

Keywords: Treasury market; structural breaks; term structure; real yields; fiscal risk; liquidity; buybacks
JEL: C22, C23, E43, E52, E58, G12

Main findings

  • High levels, ordinary slopes: nominal and real long yields exceed their 99th percentiles in 2015–26, while long-end slopes remain near historical medians.
  • A balanced common state: joint PC1 explains 95.61% of standardized common-maturity variation and splits its loading energy almost exactly between nominal and real yields.
  • Raw breaks do not survive policy conditioning: after removing persistence and effective-rate dynamics, neither curve selects a residual mean boundary.
  • The announcement response is short-lived: the long-short rotation is unusual over one and two days, but not over three or five days.

Introduction

On 17 August 2026 the U.S. Treasury's official 30-year par yield reached 5.31 percent. Two days later, Treasury announced that the maximum size of liquidity-support buybacks in the 10-to-20-year and 20-to-30-year sectors would rise from $2 billion to at least $4 billion per operation. The juxtaposition was attention-grabbing: a historically high long yield, large prospective financing needs, and an intervention explicitly directed at long-end liquidity. Yet the same facts support competing narratives. Higher long rates may compensate investors for duration supply, fiscal uncertainty, or declining convenience services; alternatively, they may reflect expected policy rates, high equilibrium real rates, inflation uncertainty, or temporary intermediation frictions.

A single benchmark yield cannot distinguish these mechanisms. They imply different cross-sectional and temporal restrictions. A common monetary or macroeconomic regime should move nominal and real curves broadly, with short-end participation. Fiscal-duration repricing should be persistent and concentrated in long real yields or term premia. A market-functioning disturbance should be sharper in liquidity-sensitive sectors and relative prices, may respond to targeted operations, and need not create a persistent common level break. This thesis uses the whole nominal and real term structures to impose that discipline.

The contribution is empirical and methodological. Empirically, the paper provides a fully reproducible description of the August episode using public data and places it in an eleven-year historical distribution. Methodologically, it treats yield curves as functional panels, separates common from curve-specific break evidence, and explicitly confronts the right-end-point problem: a retrospective break estimator cannot validate a regime that has only a few post-event observations. This avoids the common error of matching estimated dates to headlines and then naming the mechanism after the headline.

The conclusion is deliberately narrower than a causal policy claim. August 2026 is an extreme level episode driven in substantial part by real yields, not an extreme slope episode. Approximate breakevens are elevated but not exceptional. The break-date exercise documents suggestive cross-curve alignment conditional on an exploratory screen; it is not formal common-break inference. The buyback window contains a short-horizon long-versus-short rotation, but the result weakens as the window expands. The evidence therefore characterizes the episode without identifying fiscal deterioration or impaired plumbing as its unique cause.

Institutional setting and current market relevance

Treasury resumed regular buybacks in 2024 for cash management and liquidity support. Unlike Federal Reserve asset purchases, these transactions are debt-management operations: Treasury buys older securities while financing within its broader issuance program. Liquidity-support operations target less-liquid, off-the-run securities and can improve relative pricing without reducing net federal debt. This distinction matters. A purchase can alleviate a local liquidity discount even while total duration supply and fiscal borrowing remain high.

The August 2026 quarterly refunding materials placed both mechanisms in view. Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee discussions highlighted dealer balance sheets, repo conditions, transparency, central clearing, off-the-run and TIPS liquidity, and the role of buybacks. They also reported a projected $1.45 trillion FY2027–28 financing shortfall under then-current coupon sizes and privately held bill supply. The 19 August announcement increased long-end liquidity-support operations beginning 9 September. The public debate therefore was not artificially constructed by this thesis: fiscal capacity and market plumbing were simultaneously salient to the institution managing the debt.

Literature review

Term-structure representation

Nelson and Siegel (1987) provide a parsimonious representation of curve shape, while Diebold and Li (2006) interpret dynamic factors as level, slope, and curvature. Feng and Qian (2018) instead develop a genuinely functional principal-component treatment of yield curves. The baseline here is more modest: it applies ordinary SVD/PCA to a finite maturity panel and therefore is not FPCA. The functional paper motivates a possible extension, not a label for the present estimator.

Structural change and common breaks

Bai and Perron (1998, 2003) establish global estimation, testing, and confidence intervals for multiple unknown changes in linear regressions. Qu and Perron (2007) extend this logic to multivariate systems in which regression coefficients and covariance matrices may change, including partial and common breaks. Qian and Su (2016) take a different high-dimensional route: an adaptive group fused-Lasso penalty selects unknown common break dates, followed by post-Lasso and post-group-Lasso estimators whose asymptotic distributions support inference. Their sparsity formulation is especially relevant because “no break” is an estimable outcome rather than a date count imposed in advance.

The present dynamic-programming partition is deliberately not presented as any of those estimators. It minimizes within-regime factor means under an upper cap and supplies candidate dates for diagnosis. It does not estimate Qian–Su's penalized panel model, Qu–Perron's joint likelihood, confidence sets for dates, or changes in covariance dynamics. This distinction matters because persistent yield levels can make a sequence of discrete means approximate a smooth policy cycle. The paper therefore treats survival across changes, residual shape, and policy-conditioned innovations—not raw date coincidence—as the relevant evidentiary hierarchy.

Discrete versus smooth change

A structural break is not the only representation of parameter evolution. Monetary-policy cycles, equilibrium real-rate movements, and dealer-capacity adjustment may be gradual. A discrete partition can fit such evolution with many adjacent regimes and thereby generate economically tempting but statistically artificial dates. The empirical warning sign is an information criterion that continues improving at the maximum permitted break count. A complete formal comparison would estimate smooth-transition or time-varying-parameter alternatives and use a dependence-preserving bootstrap to repeat model selection. This paper does not claim that comparison is complete; instead, it reports transformations under which the discrete-boundary result should survive if it reflects an independent shock.

Supply, preferred habitat, and fiscal duration

Preferred-habitat theories imply that maturity-specific net supply affects yields when arbitrageurs have limited risk-bearing capacity. D'Amico et al. (2012/2013) and Li and Wei (2014) show that large-scale purchases operate through scarcity and duration channels, including real term-premium effects. Bi, Phillot, and Zubairy (2026) distinguish debt-expansion from maturity-extension shocks around Treasury announcements and find different propagation despite a common term-premium channel. A fiscal-duration mechanism should therefore appear not merely as “the 30-year yield is high,” but as persistent long-end or real-rate repricing after accounting for common factors.

Convenience yield, market functioning, and buybacks

Krishnamurthy and Vissing-Jorgensen (2012) show that safety and liquidity services depress Treasury yields relative to close substitutes and that this convenience value varies with supply. Lustig (2026) argues that safe-debt valuation has eroded since 2020, emphasizing relative pricing and changing investor behavior. By contrast, work on March 2020 documents temporary dislocations when sales overwhelmed dealer capacity. Fleming et al. (2021) associate Federal Reserve market-functioning purchases with improved liquidity, while D'Amico et al. (2026) distinguish flow effects on relative-price deviations from the broader stock effects of asset holdings.

Zhou (2025), IMF Working Paper 25/88, studies the Treasury's 2024 buyback program at the security level. Eligibility moderately narrows bid–ask and off-the-run spreads, while purchases raise eligible-security prices, with stronger liquidity-support effects when dealer inventories are high. Han, Longstaff, and Merrill (2007) study the earlier buyback program and are a distinct reference. Zhou's design clarifies what this thesis can and cannot infer: aggregate constant-maturity yields can reveal curve-wide and maturity-targeted responses, but cannot reproduce an eligibility-based causal estimate or directly measure liquidity.

Data and measurement

The sample comprises official U.S. Treasury daily par-yield curves from 2 January 2015 through 28 August 2026. The nominal panel contains 1-, 3-, and 6-month and 1-, 2-, 3-, 5-, 7-, 10-, 20-, and 30-year maturities. The real panel contains 5-, 7-, 10-, 20-, and 30-year maturities. After aligning dates, missing observations are carried forward for no more than two business days; incomplete curves are then removed. The final sample contains 2,916 daily and 609 Friday-weekly observations per curve family.

Par yields are appropriate for a transparent curve-level study: they are official, public, current, and span both nominal and inflation-protected securities. They are not transaction yields, zero-coupon rates, term-premium estimates, bid–ask spreads, or on/off-the-run spreads. Consequently, “fiscal risk” and “plumbing” are mechanism interpretations constrained by the data, not variables directly observed. This boundary is part of the identification strategy rather than a footnote.

Econometric framework and empirical design

Objects of interest and the identification hierarchy

Let \(c\in\{N,R\}\) index the nominal and real curves, let \(\tau\in\mathcal T_c\) belong to the observed maturity grid, and let \(y_{ct}(\tau)\) denote the par yield at week \(t\). The paper distinguishes four objects that are often conflated: the level of the curve, its residual shape, innovations to the curve, and the mapping from shocks into maturities. A fiscal-duration story, a policy-rate cycle, and a market-functioning disturbance can all move long yields, but they need not imply that the same object changes.

Nested empirical hierarchy.
  1. Describe levels and slopes without causal labels.
  2. Compress each curve and the joint nominal–real system.
  3. Screen factor means for piecewise-constant boundaries.
  4. Repeat on weekly changes and on slope/curvature after removing level.
  5. Remove factor persistence and observed policy-rate dynamics, then screen the residuals.
  6. Use the August window only as a local shape benchmark.
Interpretive proposition 1. A boundary that appears only in yield levels, disappears in changes, and disappears after conditioning on factor persistence and the effective federal funds rate is evidence of a persistent level regime, not evidence that fiscal risk or market plumbing has been separately identified.

Separate-curve and joint principal components

For each curve family, the finite maturity vector is decomposed by ordinary sample PCA, not functional PCA:

\[ y_{ct}(\tau_j)=\mu_c(\tau_j)+\sum_{k=1}^{K}f_{ctk}\phi_{ck}(\tau_j)+\varepsilon_{ctj},\qquad K=3. \tag{1} \]

The first loading is oriented positively across maturities and the second is oriented to increase with maturity. These sign conventions aid interpretation but do not impose Nelson–Siegel loadings. Because separately estimated factors do not share a coordinate system, equality of nominal and real scores is never tested.

A second decomposition stacks standardized nominal and real yields at the common 5-, 7-, 10-, 20-, and 30-year maturities:

\[ \mathbf{x}_t=\bigl[z_{Nt}(5),\ldots,z_{Nt}(30),z_{Rt}(5),\ldots,z_{Rt}(30)\bigr]'=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{g}_t+\mathbf{u}_t. \tag{2} \]

This joint PCA is descriptive, but it asks whether the dominant direction is balanced across the two curve families or concentrated in one. A genuine functional extension would smooth the covariance operator over maturity and treat sparse observation and measurement error explicitly, as in Feng and Qian (2018).

Capped piecewise-mean screening

Let \(\mathbf F_{ct}\in\mathbb R^p\) be a standardized factor vector. For a partition \(0=T_0<T_1<\cdots<T_{m+1}=T\), dynamic programming minimizes

\[ \operatorname{SSR}_c(m)=\min_{T_1,\ldots,T_m}\sum_{r=0}^{m}\sum_{t=T_r+1}^{T_{r+1}}\left\lVert\mathbf{F}_{ct}-\bar{\mathbf{F}}_{cr}\right\rVert_2^2, \qquad 0=T_0<\cdots<T_{m+1}=T. \tag{3} \]

subject to a 13-week minimum regime. The reported quasi-information criterion is

\[ \operatorname{IC}_c(m)=Tp\log\!\left(\frac{\operatorname{SSR}_c(m)}{Tp}\right)+(m+1)p\log T. \tag{4} \]

The search permits at most eight breaks. Because the criterion decreases through that upper bound, the argmin is the computational cap rather than an estimated break count. The dates are consequently called piecewise-mean screening boundaries. Qian and Su (2016), by contrast, estimate an unknown sparse set of common coefficient changes by adaptive group fused Lasso.

Diagnostic condition. A discrete-break interpretation is credible only if the selected number is interior, dates are stable to persistence-preserving resampling, and comparable boundaries remain after transforming levels into innovations or policy-conditioned residuals. The baseline fails the first condition; the additional screens evaluate the other two descriptively.

Changes, residual shape, and policy-conditioned innovations

Two transformations reduce the mechanical influence of the level factor. The changes screen applies the same partition algorithm to \(\Delta\mathbf F_{ct}\). The shape screen retains only slope and curvature. A third specification conditions on observable monetary-policy dynamics. For factor \(k\),

\[ f_{ctk}=\alpha_{ck}+\rho_{ck}f_{c,t-1,k}+\beta_{ck}r_t+\gamma_{ck}\Delta r_t+\delta_{ck}r_{t-1}+v_{ctk}. \tag{5} \]

where \(r_t\) is the New York Fed effective federal funds rate sampled weekly. The capped partition is then applied to \(\mathbf v_{ct}\). This is not a structural monetary-policy model: contemporaneous policy is not exogenous and the coefficients are descriptive projections. Its narrower purpose is to test whether the apparent boundary structure survives removal of the most obvious persistent policy component.

Conditional date-matching benchmark

For nominal candidate set \(\mathcal A\) and real candidate set \(\mathcal B\), the date-matching statistic and corrected Monte Carlo probability are

\[ M(\mathcal A,\mathcal B;h)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal A}\mathbf 1\!\left\{\min_{b\in\mathcal B}|a-b|\le h\right\},\qquad \widehat p=\frac{1+\sum_{s=1}^{S}\mathbf 1\{M^{(s)}\ge M^{\mathrm{obs}}\}}{S+1}, \tag{6} \]

where \(h=6\) weeks and \(S=20{,}000\). Each null draw contains eight dates and obeys the estimator's 13-week minimum spacing. Because the exercise conditions on estimated dates and does not re-estimate the partition under serial and cross-curve dependence, it is a calendar-overlap benchmark rather than common-break inference.

Announcement-window benchmark

The event analysis separates the 17–18 August pre-announcement move from the 18–19 August close-to-close response and reports one-, two-, three-, and five-business-day windows. The cross-maturity rotation is

\[ R_h=\Delta_h y_t(2\mathrm{y})-\Delta_h y_t(30\mathrm{y}),\qquad \Delta_h y_t(\tau)=y_t(\tau)-y_{t-h}(\tau). \tag{7} \]

Empirical ranks compare \(R_h\) with historical windows of equal length. Treasury described the scale-up as a response to strong sponsorship and high-quality offers, not as a dysfunction diagnosis. Endogenous timing, overlapping news, and the absence of security-level prices make this a shape benchmark rather than a causal event study.

Results

Factor sufficiency

The first three components explain 99.86 percent of nominal-curve and 99.99 percent of real-curve variation. The first component alone accounts for 95.67 and 97.65 percent, respectively. Nearly all cross-maturity information is therefore retained in a low-dimensional factor panel. The dominance of the level factor also warns that persistent common movements will dominate an unconstrained mean-break criterion.

Extreme levels, ordinary slopes

On 28 August the 10- and 30-year nominal yields were 4.73 and 5.22 percent, respectively—the 99.3rd and 99.6th percentiles of the 2015–26 Treasury sample. The corresponding real yields were 2.42 and 2.96 percent, at the 99.3rd and 99.5th percentiles. Yet the 30y–2y slope was 88 basis points, only the 46.0th percentile, and the 30y–10y slope was 49 basis points, the 49.8th percentile. The word “extreme” is sample-dependent: against the Federal Reserve staff GSW off-the-run nominal par curve since 1990, the same current 10- and 30-year levels are only at the 61.6th and 63.4th percentiles.

Approximate breakeven decomposition

Subtracting real par yields from nominal par yields gives an approximate breakeven measure at matched maturities. On 28 August, the 5-, 7-, 10-, 20-, and 30-year values were 2.30, 2.30, 2.31, 2.45, and 2.26 percent. Their sample percentiles ranged from 66.6 to 74.6. Inflation compensation was therefore elevated, but much less exceptional than nominal and real yield levels. Because par-yield differences also contain inflation-risk premia, TIPS liquidity, and curve-construction effects, they are not interpreted as pure expected inflation.

Common and heterogeneous structural variation

The baseline screen returns eight candidates in each curve. Four nominal candidates fall within six weeks of a real candidate. Conditional on treating the screened dates as fixed, a match count at least this large occurs in 2.11 percent of 20,000 spacing-respecting random date placements. This number does not propagate uncertainty from estimating the break dates and is therefore a descriptive randomization benchmark, not a formal common-break test. The strongest paired dates are November 2017, early 2019, the 2022 tightening transition, and late September 2023. Other dates do not coincide closely, including nominal February 2020 versus real December 2020 and the most recent nominal and real candidates.

With a five-break cap, the 13-, 26-, and 39-week minimum-regime specifications return the same dates within each curve: nominal November 2017, March 2019, January 2022, January 2023, and October 2024; real November 2017, March 2019, December 2020, June 2022, and March 2025. Stability across trimming assumptions increases confidence that the broad partitions are not artifacts of short regimes, although their economic labels remain interpretive.

Finding 2. Conditional on the exploratory screen, nominal and real level candidates align more closely than independently placed dates. The next tests show that this alignment is attributable to persistent common level dynamics rather than a residual structural shock.

The August announcement window

From 17 to 18 August, only 3 of the eventual 12 basis points of two-day 30-year decline occurred. From the 18 August close to the 19 August close, the 2-year yield was unchanged while 10-, 20-, and 30-year yields fell 6, 11, and 9 basis points. The same-day long-short rotation was 9 basis points, the 97.4th historical percentile, with a one-sided empirical probability of 0.0309. The original 17–19 rotation remains 12 basis points (p = 0.0336), but it mixes pre-announcement trading into the window and the result is not robust at three or five business days.

From 19 to 28 August, the 2-year yield rose 15 basis points, whereas the 10-, 20-, and 30-year yields rose 8, 4, and 3 basis points. The long end thus retraced only part of its announcement-window decline, but the short end moved substantially. The pattern is consistent with news that disproportionately affected targeted long maturities. It is not a causal buyback estimate: the announcement was not randomly timed, other information arrived, enlarged operations had not begun, and constant-maturity rates do not identify eligible securities.

Window sensitivity materially qualifies the result. The one-day and two-day rotations have empirical probabilities of 0.0309 and 0.0336; using non-overlapping historical windows gives 0.0309 and 0.0357. The three-day probability rises to 0.1232 and the five-day probability to 0.3360. Thus the evidence is concentrated immediately around the announcement and is not robust to a wider event window.

The announcement window is concentrated at 20–30 years, whereas the following period is led by the 2-year yield. This is the cross-sectional fact behind the targeting interpretation.

The empirical probability is below 5% only for one- and two-day windows. Evidence weakens quickly as the window expands.

Finding 3. A short-horizon targeted rotation is visible, but it is window-sensitive. This is descriptive evidence of localized repricing, not proof of a market-functioning mechanism or a conventional event-study treatment effect.

Case-study interpretation: fiscal risk or plumbing?

Benchmark case: March 2020 market dysfunction

March 2020 provides a useful visual benchmark for what an acute market-functioning disturbance looks like in the same public curves. Between 9 and 18 March, the nominal and especially real curves shifted sharply upward despite the broader flight to safety; by 30 April much of the dislocation had reversed.

The March 2020 episode shows a large, rapidly reversing cross-curve disturbance. August 2026 does not display an equally dramatic curve-wide pattern in par yields, reinforcing the need for direct liquidity measures.

Four observations weigh against a simple “fiscal steepening” verdict. First, long nominal yields are extreme but the long-short slopes are near sample medians. Second, long real yields are equally extreme, so the episode cannot be reduced to inflation compensation. Third, raw nominal and real level boundaries align, but neither curve retains a residual boundary after persistence and EFFR conditioning. Fourth, the retrospective screen does not place a new break in August 2026; its latest candidates occur earlier. The endpoint limitation prevents interpreting this absence as stability, but it also prevents declaring a new persistent fiscal regime.

Three observations make a plumbing interpretation plausible but incomplete. Treasury explicitly described the intervention as liquidity support and targeted 10-to-30-year sectors. The announcement window produced a rare long-relative-to-short rally. Existing security-level evidence shows that buyback eligibility and purchases can improve liquidity and prices. Nevertheless, this thesis lacks bid–ask, depth, repo, security-age, offer, acceptance, and eligibility observations through the event. Moreover, the 10-year response was not historically unusual, and long yields partly retraced.

The most defensible classification is therefore layered. The state entering August was a high-real-rate, high-level regime with substantial common nominal–real variation. The event response was a localized long-end rotation consistent with targeted liquidity or supply news. The data do not establish that plumbing caused the high level, nor that fiscal risk caused the event response. This separation of state and event is the paper's central interpretive contribution.

Robustness, threats to inference, and scope

Estimator and transformation checks

The low-dimensional conclusion is insensitive to retaining more than three separate-curve components, and the joint decomposition independently recovers a balanced common level. Five-break level partitions are invariant to minimum regimes of 13, 26, and 39 weeks. Splitting the sample around Treasury's 6 December 2021 curve-methodology change yields separate pre- and post-change candidates rather than making the measurement break disappear. Loading cosines above 0.95 show that broad factor directions are stable across pre- and post-2022 subsamples, although this is not a test of loading constancy.

The most consequential robustness result is transformation-based. Level boundaries weaken sharply in weekly changes, differ across shape-only panels, and vanish in both policy-conditioned residual panels. This progression is economically interpretable: a truly independent discrete disturbance should not exist solely because persistent levels were partitioned into means. The residual result is nevertheless conditional on a compact linear projection. EFFR is endogenous to the macroeconomy, the level-factor coefficient above one reflects multicollinearity and near-unit-root persistence rather than an explosive structural law, and omitted balance-sheet or inflation news may remain in the errors.

Event-window checks

The event result is strongest for the cross-maturity rotation rather than isolated yields and is similar with non-overlapping historical windows. It is unusual only over one and two business days, not over three or five. Historical ranks do not condition on macro releases or volatility and therefore are not treatment-effect standard errors. Exact announcement timestamps and intraday CUSIP prices would be required to separate announcement news from contemporaneous information.

Relation to formal break estimators

Relative to Bai–Perron, Qu–Perron, and Qian–Su, four inferential objects remain missing: data-driven selection without a binding cap; confidence sets for dates; joint estimation of partial/common and covariance breaks; and resampling that re-estimates the full factor-and-break pipeline while preserving temporal and cross-sectional dependence. A future formal specification should place nominal and real maturity coefficients in one panel, penalize group differences across adjacent weeks, and compare the selected sparse path with a smooth-transition or time-varying-parameter alternative. Until then, the capped dates are diagnostics and the residual null result is the more reliable conclusion.

Measurement and external validity

Five limitations define scope. First, par yields mix expected short rates, term premia, convenience services, and construction effects. Second, approximate breakevens include inflation-risk and TIPS-liquidity premia. Third, aggregate curves cannot observe dealer capacity or security-level relative value. Fourth, August lies at the right endpoint, where minimum-regime estimators cannot confirm a new persistent state. Fifth, the 2015–26 percentile language describes the modern matched nominal–real sample; the GSW comparison shows that current nominal levels are not historically extreme over 1990–2026.

Accordingly, the tested claim is precise: the data reject exceptional steepness, show a balanced nominal–real common level, find no residual boundaries after persistence and policy conditioning, and establish a window-sensitive long-short rotation. They do not identify a structural fiscal shock, reject liquidity effects, or estimate a buyback treatment effect.

Conclusion

This thesis examines a live Treasury-market question without treating market commentary as identification. Using 2,916 official daily nominal and real curves, it finds that August 2026 is unusual in the modern sample because rates—especially real long rates—are high, not because the curve is exceptionally steep. A joint factor assigns nearly equal loading energy to nominal and real yields. Approximate breakevens are elevated but not extreme. Most importantly, capped level boundaries disappear in both panels after conditioning on persistence and EFFR dynamics. Around the long-end buyback announcement, the 30-year yield fell unusually far relative to the 2-year over one and two days, but the result disappears in wider windows.

The combined evidence supports neither headline extreme. A fiscal-risk story overreads the level of the 30-year yield as a unique long-end slope; a plumbing story overreads an endogenous, window-sensitive announcement response. The completed empirical claim is sharper: the sample ends in a broad high-real-rate macro state with ordinary slopes and a localized short-window adjustment, while the apparent common structural breaks are not present in policy-conditioned innovations. Identifying the remaining mechanism requires security-level liquidity and supply data, term-premium decomposition, and dependence-aware common-break or smooth-change inference.
