Are fixed-loading factor models adequate for the U.S. Treasury curve?

JKV zero-coupon yields 1970–2009 · official Treasury yields 1981–2026 · month-end · figures computed in your browser from the thesis outputs
Abstract. Factor models of the yield curve — Nelson–Siegel [9], Diebold–Li [7], principal components — usually assume each maturity's sensitivity to the factors (its loading) never changes. We apply a nonparametric test for constant loadings [1] to two data sets that are never pooled.
  1. Fixed loadings are rejected. Official Treasury yields reject them with 3, 4 and 5 factors; the JKV zero-coupon panel rejects the classic three-factor model.
  2. The one exception is a power problem. JKV with four factors does not reject over 1970–2009, but every sample starting between 1977 and 1986 does: the 1970s misfit inflates the bootstrap threshold.
  3. Loadings change at a few dates, read approximately. Four-factor breaks found under every window setting are JKV 1981-08 and Treasury 1993-06; dates move with the bandwidth and run late, and a change in noise variance can produce the same signal.
  4. Rejections are robust across sub-periods, across both data sets on their 1990–2009 overlap and across bandwidths for the full-sample test (Treasury at every bandwidth; JKV four-factor only at narrow ones).
  5. Inference is sound with the bootstrap as printed. The authors' released code runs a different, diagonal bootstrap; it reproduces their tables but over-rejects badly with few maturities, so the printed covariance version is used.

2Model and test

With \(d\) factors, yields at maturity \(i\) and month \(t\) are $$ y_{it} = w_{it}'\beta_t + \varepsilon_{it}, \qquad H_0:\ w_{it} = w_i \ \text{for all } t. $$ Several ranks are tested (\(d\) = 3, 4, 5): rejecting \(d = 3\) alone could only mean three factors are too few, and a fixed model with more factors can mimic changing loadings. The test of [1] compares two estimates of the common component \(c_{it} = w_{it}'\beta_t\):

  1. Global (null). PCA of the standardized panel over all months gives one fixed loading matrix and \( \tilde c_{it} \).
  2. Local (alternative). At each month, PCA of the kernel-weighted second moment $$ \hat\Sigma_t = \frac1T\sum_{s} k_h\!\left(\tfrac{s-t}{T}\right) x_s x_s', \qquad h = \tfrac{2.35}{\sqrt{12}}\,T^{-1/5}N^{-1/10} \ \text{(Silverman's rule)}, $$ with an Epanechnikov kernel and boundary correction, gives \( \hat c_{it} \). For the full JKV sample \(h \approx 0.149\): about 71 months on each side. Every sample and every scan window recomputes \(h\) from its own length.
  3. Statistic. \( M = \frac1{NT}\sum_{i,t}(\hat c_{it}-\tilde c_{it})^2 \), bias-corrected and studentized into \( J = (T\sqrt{Nh}\,M - \hat B)/\sqrt{\hat V} \). Larger \(J\) is less like noise.
  4. Bootstrap. Artificial panels \( x^*_t = \tilde c_t + \Sigma^{1/2} z_t \) with \(\Sigma\) the global-fit residual covariance shrunk by \(0.99^{|i-j|}\) (the covariance bootstrap printed in [1]); 9,999 draws give the null distribution of \(J\). Reject when \( p = (1+\#\{J^*\ge J\})/(1+B) < 0.01 \); the smallest reportable value is \(p \le 0.0001\).

4The 1970s and the JKV four-factor result

6Break scan

A rejection says the loadings are not constant over the whole sample, not when they change. The scan splits each panel into consecutive stretches within which the test does not reject. A break is a change in how maturities co-move, not a jump in the level of rates.

6.1 The rule

  1. Grow a window month by month from 60 months, testing each with its own bandwidth at 1% (999-draw screen; 9,999-draw confirmation).
  2. Break at the first month whose inclusion makes the test reject.
  3. Retreat: if the very first window already rejects, shrink it month by month until it no longer rejects; that point is the break.
  4. Restart after a break at 60 months, using only post-break data.

Only the first and restart windows are choices; the level, the retreat and the bandwidth rule are fixed. Each window is a fresh 1% test, so a long scan meets some breaks by chance — §8 measures how often.

With N = 8–20 and T = 100–200 size is at most 5.4%; at the JKV size (N = 17, T = 480) it is 3.4–7.4%. Power is at least 90% at the JKV size. The weak spot is slow drift (design 6b1) at N = 8 — about 30% — so Treasury non-rejections would carry little weight.

9Conclusions

  1. Fixed loadings are rejected: Treasury at 3, 4 and 5 factors (6 of 6 tests on the two Treasury samples), JKV at the classic three.
  2. The exception is power: JKV's four-factor non-rejection comes from the 1970s; every start from 1977 to 1986 rejects.
  3. Loadings change at a few dates: four-factor breaks found under every window setting are JKV 1981-08 (two months after the June 1981 Fed funds peak) and Treasury 1993-06 (before the 1994 hikes). Under 0.5× or 1.5× Silverman the number and dates change, and a change in noise variance alone makes the four-factor scan reject, so dates are approximate.
  4. Robust: rejections hold across sub-periods, on the overlap of the two data sets and, for the full-sample test, across bandwidths (Treasury everywhere; JKV four-factor only at narrow ones).
  5. Sound inference: the printed covariance bootstrap controls size at our sample sizes; the code's diagonal version does not.

Implication. Models of the Treasury curve should let loadings change over time; individual break dates should be read as approximate candidates.

Open points from the week-7 work plan: the size of the full-sample test at narrow bandwidths, and why the full JKV four-factor test and the scan disagree, are not settled.
