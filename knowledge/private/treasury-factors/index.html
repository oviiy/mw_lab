Are yield-curve factor loadings constant over time?

U.S. Treasury yields, Jan 1970 – Aug 2026 · month-end · all figures computed in your browser
Abstract. Factor models of the yield curve — Nelson–Siegel, Diebold–Li [7], principal components — write yields as a few factors multiplied by a fixed loading vector. This note examines that assumption on 56 years of Treasury yields. Three principal components explain — of standardized yield variation, but loadings re-estimated through time move materially, most visibly while the short end was pinned at zero. A nonparametric test for constant loadings [1] rejects constancy for three and four factors on the thesis sample (2006–2026) but not for five, and sequential scans split the history into periods within which loadings look stable.

2Static factors

The benchmark model has one loading vector for all dates, $$ y_t(\tau_i) = w(\tau_i)'\beta_t + \varepsilon_t(\tau_i),\qquad i = 1,\dots,N, $$ estimated here by principal components of the standardized core yields over the full sample. The first three components take the familiar shapes [5]: level (all maturities move together), slope (short and long ends move in opposite directions) and curvature (the belly moves against both ends). 4Testing constancy

4.1 Method

The test of [1] compares two estimates of the common component \(c_{it} = w_{it}'\beta_t\): a static one that imposes constant loadings, and a local one that lets them vary smoothly. Under the null they differ only by estimation noise.

  1. Hypotheses. \( y_{it} = w_{it}'\beta_t + \varepsilon_{it} \), \( H_0: w_{it} = w_i \) for all \(t\). Each maturity is standardized.
  2. Static fit. Full-sample PCA gives \( \tilde c_{it} = \tilde w_i'\tilde\beta_t \).
  3. Local fit. At each date, PCA of the kernel-weighted second moment $$ \hat\Sigma_t = \frac1T\sum_{s=1}^T k_h\!\left(\tfrac{s-t}{T}\right) x_s x_s', \qquad h = \tfrac{2.35}{\sqrt{12}}\,T^{-1/5}N^{-1/10}, $$ with an Epanechnikov kernel and a boundary correction, gives \( \hat c_{it} = \hat w_{it}'\hat\beta_t \).
  4. Statistic. The mean squared gap and its bias-corrected, studentized version: $$ M = \frac1{NT}\sum_{i,t}\big(\hat c_{it}-\tilde c_{it}\big)^2, \qquad J = \frac{T\sqrt{Nh}\,M - \hat B}{\sqrt{\hat V}}. $$ \(J\) is asymptotically standard normal under \(H_0\); large values indicate moving loadings.
  5. Bootstrap. With few maturities the normal limit is unreliable, so the null is simulated: \( x^*_t = \tilde c_t + \Sigma^{1/2} z_t \), \(z_t \sim \mathcal N(0, I)\), where \(\Sigma\) is the static-residual covariance shrunk by \(0.99^{|i-j|}\). \(J\) is recomputed on each draw and \( p = (1 + \#\{J^* \ge J\})/(1 + B) \).

The thesis applies the test to month-end zero-coupon yields from September 2006 to August 2026 (17 maturities, 3M–10Y; GSW fitted zeros [4] with FRED bill rates) with B = 9,999 bootstrap draws for R = 3, 4 and 5 factors.

5Break dates

5.1 What a break means here

A rejection in §4 says the loadings are not constant over the whole sample, but not when they changed. A natural follow-up is to split the sample into consecutive segments such that, within each segment, the test does not reject constancy. The boundaries between segments are the break dates. A break is a change in how yields co-move — the shape of level, slope or curvature — not a jump in the level of rates.

5.2 How the scan works

  1. Start a window at the first month of the current segment, with a minimum length (60 months for the long sample, 100 for the 2006–2026 sample) so the test has enough data.
  2. Test the window: standardize within the window, compute \(J\) with its own bandwidth, and compare it with a 999-draw bootstrap critical value at the 1% level.
  3. Extend the window by one month and test again, as long as constancy is not rejected.
  4. Record a break at the first month whose inclusion makes the test reject; confirm it with a 9,999-draw bootstrap. The next segment starts at that month.
  5. Retreat rule. If the very first (minimum-length) window already rejects, the change happened inside it: the scan steps back to the last window end that did not reject, records that as the break, and restarts there with a shorter minimum (24 months).
  6. Stop when the remaining sample is shorter than the minimum window.

5.5 Reading break dates with care

  • Detection, not onset. A break is the first month at which the evidence crosses the 1% threshold; the underlying change may have begun earlier and been accumulating.
  • Minimum window. Changes closer together than the minimum window cannot be separated; short segments (e.g. 19 months) arise only through the retreat rule.
  • Repeated testing. Each segment involves many sequential tests at 1%; the scan is a descriptive segmentation, not a joint test with controlled overall size.
  • Monte Carlo noise. Screening uses 999 draws; a break can shift by a month or two relative to a 9,999-draw run.
  • Specification. Results depend on the number of factors, the maturities used and per-window standardization, as Figure 12 shows.

6Notes on construction

  1. One source per maturity. Stitching two sources at a fixed date would create an artificial break — exactly what §4–5 look for.
  2. Short end. The 3M and 6M CMT series begin only in September 1981. The H.15 Treasury-bill secondary-market rate covers the whole period; it is quoted on a discount basis and converted on every date to bond-equivalent yield, \( y = 365\,d/(360 - d\,n) \) with \(n\) = 91 or 182 days. Over 1981–2026 the converted bill yield differs from CMT by — (3M) and — (6M); see Figure 13. The CMT short end is not used.
  3. No interpolation. Gaps stay blank: 2Y before June 1976, 30Y before February 1977 and during its suspension (February 2002 – February 2006), 20Y in 1987–1993, 1M before July 2001.
  4. Month-end. Each month uses the last business-day observation.
  5. Par vs zero. CMT yields are par yields read from Treasury's fitted curve at benchmark tenors; the thesis panel and the 1970–2009 scan use zero-coupon yields. The two are never pooled.
  6. Implementation. The browser code reproduces the thesis statistic to six significant digits (unit-tested); bootstrap draws use a different random-number generator, so live p-values differ from the thesis by Monte Carlo noise.
