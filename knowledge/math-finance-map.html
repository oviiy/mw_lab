Why is mathematical finance built the way it is?

Derivative pricing is usually presented as a sequence of definitions — Itô's lemma, Girsanov, Feynman–Kac, Black–Scholes — each stated before the problem that motivates it. The dependency structure between them is rarely drawn, so the subject reads as a list rather than an argument.

This is the same material in dependency order. Each concept is stated with the problem it solves, an informal account, the formalism, an interactive numerical model, and an explicit list of what it requires and what requires it. Nineteen concepts, resting on roughly four independent ideas; the remainder is consequence.

Prerequisites: multivariable calculus and probability through the central limit theorem. No measure theory is assumed — where it would ordinarily be invoked, an informal equivalent is given.

How this connects

Three chains run through the diagram:

  1. The randomness spine: expectation → Brownian motion → quadratic variation → Itô. This is calculus rebuilt for objects that wiggle infinitely fast.
  2. The information spine: conditional expectation → filtration → martingale → Girsanov. This is bookkeeping for what you know when.
  3. The pricing spine: no-arbitrage → risk-neutral measure → Feynman–Kac → Black–Scholes. This is the claim that a hedgeable payoff has exactly one price.

The two spines meet at Itô + martingale, and the meeting point is the Black–Scholes PDE.

Common confusion

Students often assume the difficulty is the stochastic calculus. It usually isn't. The stochastic calculus is one strange fact (quadratic variation) plus ordinary Taylor expansion. The genuinely conceptual difficulty is the measure change — accepting that we deliberately price using probabilities nobody believes.

1. Random variables, expectation as a "fair price", and what volatility actually measures

Why this matters

Every pricing formula in this entire field ends in an expectation. If "expectation" means nothing more to you than "the average", the formulas will always look like arbitrary integrals rather than the answer to a question someone actually asked.

The intuition

Imagine a friend offers you a coupon: they'll flip a fair coin, and pay you 100 dollars on heads and nothing on tails. What should you pay for that coupon? Fifty. Not because fifty is a possible outcome — it never happens — but because at fifty, neither side wants to switch places with the other. That is the whole idea. Expectation is the number that makes a gamble a non-event: the price at which the trade is boring.

Now change the coupon: 51 dollars on heads, 49 on tails. Same expectation, fifty. But it is obviously a different object — one is a coin flip, the other is nearly a bank deposit. Expectation can't see the difference, so we need a second number that measures how far outcomes spread around the fair price. That's variance, and its square root, volatility, is in the same units as the payoff itself, which is why practitioners quote volatility and not variance.

Here is the part that matters for finance and gets said badly everywhere: volatility is dispersion, not risk. A lottery ticket that pays either 0 or 1,000,000 has enormous volatility and essentially no risk that you care about, because you can only lose your ticket price. Volatility is symmetric and indifferent to whether you're long or short. Risk is a statement about your exposure and your ruin. The reason volatility dominates this field anyway is not that it captures risk — it's that, for the Gaussian machinery we're about to build, the mean and the volatility are the entire distribution. Nothing else is left to say.

The formal math

A random variable \(X\) is a function from outcomes to numbers; a probability measure \(\mathbb{P}\) assigns weight to sets of outcomes. Expectation is the weighted average:

$$\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[X]=\int_\Omega X(\omega)\,d\mathbb{P}(\omega) \;=\;\begin{cases}\sum_i x_i\,p_i & \text{discrete}\\[4pt] \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x\,f(x)\,dx & \text{density }f\end{cases}$$

Variance and volatility:

$$\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb{E}\big[(X-\mathbb{E}X)^2\big]=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-(\mathbb{E}X)^2, \qquad \sigma=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}$$

Two properties used throughout, and frequently conflated: expectation is linear unconditionally, variance is additive only in the absence of correlation.

$$\mathbb{E}[aX+bY]=a\mathbb{E}[X]+b\mathbb{E}[Y],\qquad \operatorname{Var}(aX+bY)=a^2\operatorname{Var}X+b^2\operatorname{Var}Y+2ab\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)$$

The superscript \(\mathbb{P}\) looks like pedantry now. In Tier 2 it becomes the single most important symbol on the page, because we will compute expectations under a measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) that is deliberately not the real one.

Going deeper: why "fair price" and "expectation" come apart in the real world

If expectation were really the price of every gamble, nobody would buy insurance and nobody would sell it — the premium exceeds the expected payout, so the buyer is overpaying by construction. People pay anyway because a certain small loss and an uncertain large one are not interchangeable to them. Formally, agents maximise \(\mathbb{E}[u(W)]\) for a concave utility \(u\), and concavity means \(\mathbb{E}[u(X)]

Derivative pricing performs a spectacular dodge around all of this: if a payoff can be replicated by trading the underlying, its price is pinned by no-arbitrage regardless of anyone's utility. That dodge is the entire content of Tier 2, and it's why you will never see a utility function again after this box.

Common confusion

"High volatility means high risk, so a high-vol option should be worth less." Backwards. For an option holder, volatility is good: your downside is capped at the premium and your upside isn't. Spread the distribution and you add probability mass to deep-in-the-money outcomes while the deep-out-of-the-money outcomes all collapse to the same zero. Vega is positive for both calls and puts, and that surprises nearly everyone the first time.

2. Conditional expectation and information — "what you know by time t"

Why this matters

A price today is a forecast made with today's information. Every single pricing formula in this field is a conditional expectation, and the thing being conditioned on — the information set — is the object that martingales, hedging, and the whole risk-neutral apparatus are secretly about.

The intuition

Think of a long football match with live betting. Before kickoff, the fair price of a "home team wins" contract is some number based on form and odds. Ten minutes in, the home team scores; the price jumps. Nothing about the eventual outcome changed — the match will end how it ends. What changed is what you know. The fair price is a moving best guess, and it moves precisely when information arrives.

So we need a way to talk about "the state of knowledge at time \(t\)". Picture it as a growing folder. At time 0 the folder is empty; by time \(t\) it contains the entire history of everything observable up to \(t\); by time \(T\) it contains everything. The folder only ever grows — you cannot un-learn — and that monotone family of folders is called a filtration, written \(\{\mathcal{F}_t\}\). The intimidating notation is doing something completely mundane: it's a clock attached to an information set.

Given the folder, \(\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{F}_t]\) is "my best guess of \(X\), using everything I know at time \(t\)". Three properties, stated before the formalism:

  • It is not a number. It's a random variable, because which folder-contents you end up with is itself random. Today I don't know what I'll know tomorrow.
  • It is a projection — geometrically, the closest thing to \(X\) that you could actually compute with the information you have. That's not an analogy; in \(L^2\) it is literally orthogonal projection onto the subspace of \(\mathcal{F}_t\)-measurable variables.
  • Averaging your guesses gives back the original average: tower property. If I tell you today what I expect to believe tomorrow, that has to equal what I believe today — otherwise I should just update now.

The formal math

A filtration is an increasing family of \(\sigma\)-algebras \(\mathcal{F}_s\subseteq\mathcal{F}_t\) for \(s\le t\). Conditional expectation \(Y=\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{F}_t]\) is the unique (a.s.) \(\mathcal{F}_t\)-measurable random variable satisfying

$$\int_A Y\,d\mathbb{P}=\int_A X\,d\mathbb{P}\qquad\text{for every }A\in\mathcal{F}_t.$$

In words: on any event you could have identified by time \(t\), the guess and the truth have the same average. The workhorse properties:

$$\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{F}_t]\big]=\mathbb{E}[X]}_{\text{tower}},\qquad \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[ZX\mid\mathcal{F}_t]=Z\,\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{F}_t]}_{Z\ \mathcal{F}_t\text{-measurable}},\qquad \mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{F}_T]\mid\mathcal{F}_t\big]=\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{F}_t]$$

"Taking out what is known" is the second one, and it is the formal version of: things you have already observed are constants to you.

Going deeper: measurability, in one paragraph, without measure theory

"\(Y\) is \(\mathcal{F}_t\)-measurable" translates to: \(Y\) is a quantity you could physically write down at time \(t\). If two possible futures are indistinguishable using the information in \(\mathcal{F}_t\), then \(Y\) must take the same value in both — you can't compute something that depends on data you don't have. That's the entire content of the word.

The reason it earns a formal definition is that it's exactly the constraint a trading strategy must obey. A hedge \(\Delta_t\) must be \(\mathcal{F}_t\)-measurable, i.e. adapted. Drop that requirement and you can "hedge" using tomorrow's price, which prices every option at its discounted payoff and makes the theory vacuous. When we get to Itô integrals, the reason the integrand is evaluated at the left endpoint of each interval is this and nothing else.

This is why the tower property is not a triviality: \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{F}_t]]=\mathbb{E}[X]\) is averaging a random guess over all the ways the world could reveal itself. Students who read it as "the average of a number is the number" find martingales mysterious two sections later.

3. The Markov property — the future only cares where you are, not how you got there

Why this matters

Without it, the value of an option would depend on the entire history of the stock price, and pricing would be an infinite-dimensional problem. Markov is the assumption that turns "value of a path" into "value of a point", and it is the reason a PDE in \((S,t)\) — two variables — can price a derivative at all.

The intuition

You're playing a board game. It's your turn. To decide your move, do you need to know the sequence of dice rolls that got the pieces where they are? No — you need the board. The board is the state, and the history is decoration. A process is Markov when its state summarises everything the future cares about.

Compare two ways a stock could behave. In the first, tomorrow's return distribution depends only on today's price — Markov. In the second, tomorrow's return distribution depends on whether the stock has been trending up for a week — not Markov, because the folder from the previous section can no longer be compressed into the single number \(S_t\); you'd need the trend too.

Here is the crucial and constantly-missed subtlety: Markov is not a property of the world, it's a property of your state variable. A momentum-driven stock is non-Markov in \(S_t\) and perfectly Markov in the pair \((S_t,\text{trend}_t)\). Almost all of Tier 4 is exactly this move — the model breaks, so you widen the state until Markov comes back. Heston adds variance to the state. Short-rate models add \(r\). It's the same trick every time, and once you see it, Tier 4 stops being a list of unrelated models.

The formal math

\(X\) is Markov with respect to \(\{\mathcal{F}_t\}\) if for any bounded measurable \(h\) and \(s\le t\):

$$\mathbb{E}\big[h(X_t)\mid\mathcal{F}_s\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[h(X_t)\mid X_s\big]$$

The whole folder on the left; a single number on the right. Consequently the conditional expectation is a deterministic function of the current state,

$$\mathbb{E}\big[h(X_T)\mid\mathcal{F}_t\big]=v(t,X_t)\quad\text{for some function }v,$$

and the transition densities compose (Chapman–Kolmogorov):

$$p(s,x;u,z)=\int p(s,x;t,y)\,p(t,y;u,z)\,dy,\qquad sThat middle display is the entire reason Feynman–Kac exists. A conditional expectation over paths collapses into a function \(v(t,x)\) of two arguments — and a function of two arguments is the sort of thing a PDE can solve for.

Common confusion

"Markov means increments are independent" — no, that's a strictly stronger condition that Brownian motion happens to satisfy. An Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process (Vasicek) is Markov but its increments are strongly dependent: being high now makes the next move likely negative. Markov says the present state is a sufficient statistic for the future; it says nothing about increments being unrelated to each other.

Tier 1

Stochastic calculus core

Ordinary calculus assumes local linearity: magnify a smooth curve far enough and it approaches a straight line. Brownian motion violates this, and Itô calculus is the repair. Four results, of which the second — quadratic variation — is the only genuinely non-classical one.

4. Brownian motion — the infinitely jagged random walk

Why this matters

Brownian motion is the noise term in essentially every model you will meet. If you don't have a physical feel for how weird it is — continuous everywhere, differentiable nowhere, infinite length in any finite interval — then Itô's lemma will look like a typo in the chain rule.

The intuition

Start with something you already trust: a coin-flip random walk. Every second you step \(+1\) or \(-1\). After \(n\) seconds the displacement is of order \(\pm\sqrt{n}\), not \(\pm n\), because the steps partially cancel. That \(\sqrt{n}\) scaling governs everything below.

Now speed the walk up. Steps every millisecond, then every microsecond. To stop the path from either freezing at zero or exploding, you have to shrink the step size as you shrink the time step — and the \(\sqrt{n}\) scaling tells you exactly how: if time shrinks by a factor of \(N\), the step must shrink by \(\sqrt{N}\), not \(N\). Take the limit and you get Brownian motion.

That square root is the entire source of the strangeness. Over a time interval \(\Delta t\), the typical move is of size \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\). So the difference quotient — the thing whose limit would be a derivative — is

$$\frac{\Delta W}{\Delta t}\sim\frac{\sqrt{\Delta t}}{\Delta t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta t}}\longrightarrow\infty.$$

The path is continuous (moves go to zero) but its slope blows up everywhere (moves go to zero too slowly). A Brownian path is a curve that never jumps and never has a tangent. Zoom in on any piece of it and it looks statistically identical to the whole — self-similar jaggedness, all the way down. There is no scale at which it flattens out. Every intuition you have from smooth curves is now invalid, and Tier 1 is the process of rebuilding calculus without it.

A further consequence: because the standard deviation grows like \(\sqrt{t}\), the envelope of possible paths is a parabola on its side, not a cone. Doubling the horizon doesn't double your uncertainty — it multiplies it by 1.41. That is why a one-year option costs far less than twelve one-month options.

The formal math

A standard Brownian motion (Wiener process) \(W=\{W_t\}_{t\ge0}\) is defined by four properties:

  1. \(W_0=0\);
  2. independent increments: for \(t_0<t_1<\dots<t_n\), the increments \(W_{t_{i+1}}-W_{t_i}\) are independent;
  3. Gaussian, stationary increments: \(W_t-W_s\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\,t-s)\) — note the variance is \(t-s\), so the standard deviation is \(\sqrt{t-s}\);
  4. continuous paths almost surely.

Immediate consequences: \(\mathbb{E}[W_t]=0\), \(\operatorname{Var}(W_t)=t\), \(\operatorname{Cov}(W_s,W_t)=\min(s,t)\), and self-similarity \(W_{ct}\overset{d}{=}\sqrt{c}\,W_t\).

Non-differentiability, made precise: with probability one, the path \(t\mapsto W_t\) is nowhere differentiable, and moreover has unbounded total variation on every interval —

$$\sup_{\Pi}\sum_i\big|W_{t_{i+1}}-W_{t_i}\big|=\infty\quad\text{a.s.}$$

Arithmetic Brownian motion adds drift and scale; geometric Brownian motion (the stock model) applies it to the logarithm:

$$dX_t=\mu\,dt+\sigma\,dW_t,\qquad\qquad dS_t=\mu S_t\,dt+\sigma S_t\,dW_t.$$

Read \(dS_t=\mu S_t dt+\sigma S_t dW_t\) as: "over the next instant, the stock earns \(\mu\,dt\) in expectation and gets kicked by an independent Gaussian of size \(\sigma\sqrt{dt}\) — both proportional to the current price, because a 1% move means the same thing at 50 dollars and at 500."

Going deeper: why Gaussian and not some other distribution?

It's forced, not chosen. If you insist on (a) continuous paths, (b) independent stationary increments, and (c) finite variance, then the increments must be Gaussian — the CLT does it to you, since any increment over \([0,t]\) is the sum of \(n\) i.i.d. increments over \([0,t/n]\) for every \(n\). This is Lévy's characterisation territory.

To escape the Gaussian you must drop one of the three assumptions. Drop continuity and you get jump processes. Drop finite variance and you get stable/Lévy models. Drop independent increments and you get stochastic volatility. That's Tier 4 in a sentence: each model is a different assumption you decided to give up.

Common confusion

"\(dW\) is a very small number, like \(dt\)." No — and this single misconception causes more damage than any other in stochastic calculus. \(dW\) is of order \(\sqrt{dt}\), which is enormously bigger than \(dt\). If \(dt=10^{-6}\), then \(\sqrt{dt}=10^{-3}\): a thousand times larger. That is precisely why \((dW)^2\approx dt\) survives into the next order while \((dt)^2\) does not, and it's why Itô's lemma has a term the ordinary chain rule doesn't.

5. Quadratic variation: why \((dW)^2\) behaves like \(dt\)

Why this matters

This is the least intuitive result in the subject and the one with the widest reach. It is why Itô's lemma carries a correction term, why volatility is observable from a single path, why delta-hedging leaks money in a specific and quantifiable way, and why gamma commands a price. Most of the apparent strangeness of stochastic calculus reduces to this one fact.

The intuition

Take an ordinary smooth function \(f\) and chop \([0,T]\) into \(n\) pieces. Each increment is about \(f'\cdot\Delta t\), so each squared increment is about \((f')^2(\Delta t)^2\), and the sum of \(n\) of them is \(n\cdot(T/n)^2=T^2/n\to 0\). For smooth functions, squared increments vanish. This is exactly why ordinary Taylor expansions stop at first order: the second-order terms are negligible.

Now do it for Brownian motion. Each increment is about \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\) in size, so each squared increment is about \(\Delta t\), and the sum of \(n\) of them is \(n\cdot(T/n)=T\). It doesn't vanish. It converges to \(T\).

Two distinct facts are in play, and they are worth separating:

  • The limit isn't zero. A second-order term that refuses to disappear is precisely the ingredient the ordinary chain rule assumes away. This is where the Itô correction comes from.
  • The limit isn't random. This is the genuinely shocking part. You'd expect a sum of \(n\) random things to itself be random. But squaring makes every term positive, so nothing cancels; instead the law of large numbers takes over and the fluctuations wash out. Each \((\Delta W_i)^2\) has mean \(\Delta t\) and variance \(2(\Delta t)^2\), so summing \(n\) of them gives mean \(T\) and variance \(2T^2/n\to0\). The randomness dies faster than the mean accumulates.

The consequence is strong: volatility is not a statistical estimate, it is a measurement. In this model, if you could observe a stock's path continuously for one second, you could read off \(\sigma\) exactly — no confidence interval, no sample size. Drift, by contrast, is nearly impossible to estimate even with decades of data, because drift is a first-order effect drowning in \(\sqrt{t}\) noise. That asymmetry — vol is observable, drift isn't — is quietly why the entire pricing theory is engineered to depend on \(\sigma\) and to make \(\mu\) disappear.

The slogan \((dW)^2=dt\) is shorthand for: in any expression, a squared Brownian increment may be replaced by \(dt\), and the error vanishes in the limit. It is not an approximation being waved through; it is an identity in the limit.

The formal math

For a partition \(\Pi=\{0=t_0<\dots<t_n=T\}\) with mesh \(\|\Pi\|\to0\), the quadratic variation is

$$[W]_T=\lim_{\|\Pi\|\to0}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\big(W_{t_{i+1}}-W_{t_i}\big)^2 = T\quad\text{in }L^2\text{ (and a.s. for refining partitions).}$$

Proof sketch in two lines. Let \(Z_i=(\Delta W_i)^2-\Delta t_i\). Then \(\mathbb{E}[Z_i]=0\) and, since \(\Delta W_i\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Delta t_i)\) has fourth moment \(3(\Delta t_i)^2\), \(\operatorname{Var}(Z_i)=2(\Delta t_i)^2\). The \(Z_i\) are independent, so

$$\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_i(\Delta W_i)^2-T\Big)^2\Big]=\sum_i 2(\Delta t_i)^2\le 2\|\Pi\|\,T\;\longrightarrow\;0.$$

The Itô multiplication table that falls out, and that you will use mechanically forever:

$$dW\cdot dW=dt,\qquad dW\cdot dt=0,\qquad dt\cdot dt=0,\qquad dW^{(1)}\cdot dW^{(2)}=\rho\,dt$$

For a general Itô process \(dX=\mu_t\,dt+\sigma_t\,dW_t\), the same computation gives

$$d[X]_t=\sigma_t^2\,dt\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad [X]_T=\int_0^T\sigma_t^2\,dt$$

The drift contributes nothing to quadratic variation. Read that again — it is the mathematical statement of "vol is observable, drift isn't", and it will reappear as the punchline of Girsanov: you can change the drift freely, but you can never change the quadratic variation, so two measures that disagree on \(\sigma\) can't both be describing the same paths.

Going deeper: where practitioners meet this every single day

Realised variance. \(\sum_i(\log S_{t_{i+1}}-\log S_{t_i})^2\) computed on 5-minute bars is an estimate of \(\int\sigma^2dt\). Variance swaps pay off on exactly this sum. An entire product class exists because quadratic variation is measurable.

Hedging P&L. A delta-hedged option position's profit over \([t,t+dt]\) is \(\tfrac12\Gamma\big((dS)^2-\sigma_{\text{impl}}^2S^2dt\big)\): you are long realised variance and short implied variance. Gamma scalping is trading quadratic variation directly. See the Greeks.

Why hedging is never perfect. You rebalance \(n\) times, not continuously. The leftover terms are exactly the \(Z_i\) above, with total variance \(\propto 1/n\) — so hedging error decays like \(1/\sqrt{n}\). Continuous-time replication is a limit you approach and never reach.

Common confusion

"\((dW)^2=dt\) means \(dW=\sqrt{dt}\), so randomness has disappeared." No. Individual increments remain fully random: \(\Delta W\) is a mean-zero Gaussian that is just as likely negative as positive. What's deterministic is the accumulated sum of squares, because squaring destroys the signs so there's nothing left to cancel, and then the LLN finishes the job. A single \((\Delta W)^2\) is a random variable with mean \(\Delta t\) — it's only the limit of the sum that is a constant.

6. Itô's lemma — the chain rule plus a correction term

Why this matters

An option's value is a function of the stock price. To say anything at all about how the option moves, you need to push a random process through a function — and the ordinary chain rule gives the wrong answer. Itô's lemma is the right answer, and it is the single most-used tool in the field.

The intuition

Ordinary chain rule: if \(x\) moves by \(dx\), then \(f(x)\) moves by \(f'(x)\,dx\). Why is that legitimate? Because Taylor says \(df=f'dx+\tfrac12f''(dx)^2+\dots\), and for a smooth \(x(t)\) the term \((dx)^2\) is of order \((dt)^2\) — vanishingly small compared to \(dt\). So you drop it. The chain rule is a first-order approximation that happens to be exact in the limit.

Now feed in Brownian motion. From the previous section, \((dW)^2=dt\) — not \((dt)^2\). The second-order term is the same order as the first-order \(dt\) term. You are no longer permitted to drop it. Keep it, and you have Itô's lemma. That's the whole thing. Itô's lemma is the chain rule with the second-order term retained, because for Brownian motion it doesn't die.

Now: what does the extra term mean? It's a convexity adjustment, and there's a picture for it. Suppose \(f\) is convex (curving upward, \(f''>0\)) and \(x\) sits at a point. Jiggle \(x\) symmetrically up and down. The up-move raises \(f\) by more than the down-move lowers it — because the curve bends away from the chord. So symmetric noise in \(x\) produces an upward drift in \(f\). That asymmetry has to appear somewhere in the equation, and \(\tfrac12f''\sigma^2dt\) is where.

This is Jensen's inequality happening in continuous time, once per instant, forever. It's also exactly why an option (a convex function of the stock) earns money from the stock wobbling, and why the person who sold it has to be paid for that — see gamma and theta. The Itô correction term and the price of an option are the same phenomenon viewed from two angles.

The formal math

Let \(dX_t=\mu_t\,dt+\sigma_t\,dW_t\) and let \(f(t,x)\) be \(C^{1,2}\). Taylor-expand and keep everything of order \(dt\) or larger:

$$df=\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}dt+\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}dX+\frac12\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x^2}\underbrace{(dX)^2}_{=\ \sigma^2dt}+\underbrace{\dots}_{o(dt)}$$

Substituting \(dX=\mu\,dt+\sigma\,dW\) and \((dX)^2=\sigma^2dt\):

$$\boxed{\;df(t,X_t)=\Big(\underbrace{\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}}_{\text{time decay}}+\underbrace{\mu_t\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}}_{\text{ordinary chain rule}}+\underbrace{\frac12\sigma_t^2\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x^2}}_{\textbf{Itô correction}}\Big)dt+\underbrace{\sigma_t\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,dW_t}_{\text{noise, scaled by slope}}\;}$$

Worked example 1 — the one that makes it concrete. Take \(f(x)=x^2\) and \(X=W\): \(f'=2W\), \(f''=2\), so \(d(W^2)=2W\,dW+dt\). Integrating,

$$\int_0^T W_t\,dW_t=\tfrac12W_T^2-\tfrac12 T.$$

Ordinary calculus would have said \(\tfrac12W_T^2\). The \(-\tfrac12T\) is not a rounding error — it's what makes the left side a martingale (mean zero), as it must be, since \(\mathbb{E}[W_T^2]=T\). Check it: without the correction, an integral against \(dW\) would have had non-zero mean, which is impossible.

Worked example 2 — geometric Brownian motion, the one you'll use. With \(dS=\mu S\,dt+\sigma S\,dW\), take \(f=\log S\), so \(f'=1/S\), \(f''=-1/S^2\):

$$d(\log S_t)=\Big(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma^2\Big)dt+\sigma\,dW_t \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad S_T=S_0\exp\!\Big[\big(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma^2\big)T+\sigma W_T\Big]$$

That \(-\tfrac12\sigma^2\) is the most consequential minus sign in finance. The stock grows at rate \(\mu\) in expectation (\(\mathbb{E}[S_T]=S_0e^{\mu T}\)), but the typical (median) path grows at only \(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma^2\). Volatility is a drag on compounded growth. It is the same reason a portfolio that gains 50% then loses 50% is down 25%. It will show up again inside \(d_2\) in the Black–Scholes formula.

Going deeper: the Itô integral, and why the left endpoint matters

\(\int_0^T H_t\,dW_t\) can't be defined pathwise as a Riemann–Stieltjes integral, because \(W\) has infinite total variation. Instead it's defined as an \(L^2\) limit of left-endpoint Riemann sums: \(\sum_i H_{t_i}(W_{t_{i+1}}-W_{t_i})\).

For smooth integrators the choice of evaluation point is irrelevant. Here it changes the answer. Using the midpoint instead gives the Stratonovich integral, which obeys the ordinary chain rule (no correction term!) but is not a martingale.

Finance chooses Itô for a reason that is economic, not mathematical: \(H_{t_i}\) is your position, and you must choose it before seeing the move \(W_{t_{i+1}}-W_{t_i}\). Left-endpoint evaluation is the mathematical encoding of "no peeking" — of adaptedness. Stratonovich lets your position depend slightly on the move you're about to profit from, which is why it's the right convention in physics and the wrong one in trading.

The payoff for choosing Itô: every Itô integral of an adapted, square-integrable integrand is a martingale. That single fact is the engine of risk-neutral pricing — it's how "the \(dW\) term has zero expectation" becomes a rigorous statement.

Visualize it

Pick a function and watch the two predictions diverge. The teal path is the true \(f(W_t)\); the orange is what the naive chain rule (\(\int f'dW\) alone) predicts. Their gap is the accumulated Itô term \(\tfrac12\int f''\sigma^2dt\), drawn in the lower panel — and notice it is smooth and monotone for convex \(f\). The correction isn't noise. It's a systematic bias with a sign you can predict from the curvature.

Common confusion

Believing the Itô term is a small correction you could ignore in a pinch. It's the same order as everything else you kept, and it's often the dominant economic effect. For a one-year at-the-money option at 30% vol, the \(\tfrac12\sigma^2\) term is 4.5% — comparable to the entire risk-free rate. And in Black–Scholes the \(\mu\,\partial_x f\) term (the "real" chain rule piece) gets completely deleted by the hedging argument, while the Itô term survives and becomes the whole price. The correction isn't a footnote to the theory; it is the theory.

7. Martingales — the fair game, and why pricing needs one

Why this matters

"No free lunch" sounds like an economic slogan. A martingale is what that slogan looks like when you write it as mathematics — and the entire risk-neutral pricing framework is the statement that discounted prices must be martingales under some measure.

The intuition

You're at a roulette table with a hypothetical zero-free wheel. Your wealth after each spin is random, but your expected wealth after the next spin, given everything you've seen so far, equals your wealth right now. The game is fair. That's a martingale: a process whose best forecast of its own future is its present value.

Two immediate consequences. First, a martingale has no trend — not "no trend on average across the population" but "no trend conditional on any history whatsoever". Even if you've won twenty times in a row, your expected next-spin change is zero. Second, and much less obvious, you cannot beat a martingale with a clever strategy. Any betting system built from adapted (non-clairvoyant) bets applied to a fair game produces another fair game. Martingale-in, martingale-out. Doubling strategies, stop-losses, momentum rules — all provably useless against a genuine martingale. This is the mathematical death of technical analysis in an efficient market, and it's also the reason the pricing theory works: if no strategy can generate expected profit, then prices are pinned.

Now, real stocks are obviously not martingales. They trend upward — equity risk premium, the whole reason anyone owns them. Two separate fixes are needed and students routinely conflate them:

  • Discounting. Even a riskless bank account grows at \(r\), so no price process can be a martingale in raw terms. Measure everything in units of the money-market account: \(\tilde S_t=e^{-rt}S_t\). Now the bank account is flat by construction.
  • Changing the measure. Discounting removes \(r\); it doesn't remove the risk premium \(\mu-r\). To kill that, you change which probabilities you use — see Girsanov. The measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) is defined as exactly the one that makes \(\tilde S\) a martingale.

So "risk-neutral measure" and "the measure under which discounted prices are fair games" are the same phrase. If you hold that, Tier 2 is mostly bookkeeping.

The formal math

\(M\) is a martingale with respect to \((\{\mathcal{F}_t\},\mathbb{P})\) if it's adapted, integrable (\(\mathbb{E}|M_t|<\infty\)), and

$$\mathbb{E}\big[M_t\mid\mathcal{F}_s\big]=M_s\quad\text{for all }s\le t.$$

Sub-/super-martingale replace \(=\) with \(\ge\)/\(\le\) (a submartingale trends up — a stock; a supermartingale trends down — your wealth in a casino).

The Itô test. For \(dM=\mu_t\,dt+\sigma_t\,dW_t\), \(M\) is a martingale iff the drift vanishes: \(\mu_t\equiv0\) (plus integrability). This turns "is it a martingale?" into "expand with Itô, read the \(dt\) coefficient". Use it constantly.

Optional stopping. For a bounded stopping time \(\tau\), \(\mathbb{E}[M_\tau]=M_0\) — you cannot improve a fair game by choosing when to quit.

Martingale representation. If \(M\) is a martingale with respect to the filtration generated by \(W\), then there exists an adapted \(H\) with

$$M_t=M_0+\int_0^t H_s\,dW_s.$$

That last theorem is doing enormous, usually invisible work. It says every martingale in a Brownian world is a stochastic integral — i.e. every fair-game payoff can be manufactured by trading. \(H_s\) is the hedge ratio, and it is the abstract existence proof behind delta hedging. Market completeness is exactly this theorem.

Going deeper: local martingales, and the doubling strategy that shouldn't work

"Drift zero implies martingale" needs an integrability side-condition, and the failure mode is economically real. Consider the classic doubling strategy: bet 1; if you lose, bet 2; then 4, and so on until your first win, then stop. You win 1 with probability one. Free money?

Only if you have unbounded credit and unbounded time. The strategy is a local martingale that isn't a true martingale — optional stopping fails because the required capital is unbounded. This is why arbitrage-free theory insists on admissible strategies with a floor on wealth. The technical condition is quarantining a genuine economic loophole, not fussiness.

Same phenomenon in models: under some parameter choices a stock price process is a strict local martingale under \(\mathbb{Q}\), and put-call parity breaks. That's the mathematics of a bubble.

Visualize it

Simulate many paths and watch the running cross-sectional mean. With drift set to zero the mean stays pinned at the start value no matter how wild individual paths get — that's the martingale property, visible. Now turn on the betting strategy (double after losses). The drift is still exactly zero — but look at what happens to the readout: the standard deviation explodes and the worst path goes deeply negative, so the sample mean now jitters around 100 far too wildly to look flat. That is the honest picture. The strategy created no edge; it converted a tame distribution into one with a monstrous tail, and it only "wins" because we let it borrow without limit.

  • Binomial pricing — the risk-neutral probability \(q\) is chosen to make the tree a martingale.

The Fundamental Theorems of Asset Pricing, in plain English: (1) no arbitrage \(\iff\) at least one martingale measure exists; (2) the market is complete (every payoff is hedgeable) \(\iff\) that measure is unique. Both theorems are statements about martingales, which is why this concept sits at the hinge of the whole map.

Common confusion

"Stocks aren't martingales, so martingale pricing must be wrong." Correct premise, wrong conclusion. Stocks aren't martingales under the real-world measure \(\mathbb{P}\) — they'd better not be, or nobody would hold them. The claim is that the discounted price is a martingale under a different, artificial measure \(\mathbb{Q}\). No one asserts \(\mathbb{Q}\) describes reality. It's a computational device that no-arbitrage guarantees exists. Keeping \(\mathbb{P}\) and \(\mathbb{Q}\) mentally separate — real world for risk management, \(\mathbb{Q}\) for pricing — is the single biggest conceptual step in Tier 2.

8. Girsanov's theorem — changing the drift you believe in

Why this matters

We're about to price options using probabilities that are provably wrong. Girsanov is the theorem that says this is legal — that you can reweight the probabilities of paths to install whatever drift you like, without changing which paths are possible.

The intuition

Start with something concrete. A coin lands heads with probability 0.6. I hand you a different set of odds — 0.5/0.5 — and ask you to compute an average using those instead. You can. Nothing stops you. You've changed the measure: the same two outcomes, reweighted. The only rule is that you can't assign zero weight to something that could happen, or positive weight to something that can't. Two measures that agree on what's possible while disagreeing on what's likely are called equivalent.

Now do it in continuous time. Take a Brownian motion with drift: \(X_t=\mu t+\sigma W_t\). Under the real measure \(\mathbb{P}\), paths that trend upward are the likely ones. Girsanov says: I can write down a reweighting of path-probabilities under which \(X\) has drift zero — or drift \(r\), or any drift I choose. Upward paths still occur, they're just assigned less weight. The set of possible paths is untouched; only their likelihoods move.

The geometric picture: a Brownian path is a cloud of possible trajectories. Girsanov doesn't push the cloud sideways — it makes one side of the cloud heavier and the other lighter, and the cloud's centre of mass slides as a result. The cloud's width, though, cannot move at all: volatility is invariant under measure change, and that is a hard theorem, not a convention. Why? Because quadratic variation is a pathwise limit — computed from a single realised path with no probabilities anywhere in the calculation. Reweighting probabilities cannot change a number that never consulted the probabilities. Drift is an opinion; volatility is a fact.

That one sentence explains an enormous amount. It explains why option prices don't depend on \(\mu\) but depend critically on \(\sigma\). It explains why traders argue about volatility and never about expected return when quoting an option. It explains why you can calibrate \(\sigma\) from market prices but can never back out \(\mu\).

The reweighting factor is called the Radon–Nikodym derivative, \(d\mathbb{Q}/d\mathbb{P}\). Think of it as a density on path-space: a number attached to each path saying "multiply this path's probability by this much." To shift drift by \(-\theta\sigma\), the recipe tips weight against paths that went up, exponentially in how far they went.

The formal math

Let \(W\) be \(\mathbb{P}\)-Brownian and \(\theta_t\) adapted (satisfying Novikov's condition \(\mathbb{E}\exp(\tfrac12\int_0^T\theta^2dt)<\infty\)). Define the stochastic exponential

$$Z_t=\exp\Big(-\int_0^t\theta_s\,dW_s-\frac12\int_0^t\theta_s^2\,ds\Big),\qquad \frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\mathbb{P}}\Big|_{\mathcal{F}_T}=Z_T.$$

Then under \(\mathbb{Q}\), the process

$$W^{\mathbb{Q}}_t=W_t+\int_0^t\theta_s\,ds$$

is a standard Brownian motion. Equivalently \(dW_t=dW^{\mathbb{Q}}_t-\theta_t\,dt\): you have installed a drift of \(-\theta\,dt\) into the old Brownian motion.

Applied to a stock. With \(dS=\mu S\,dt+\sigma S\,dW\), choose the market price of risk

$$\theta=\frac{\mu-r}{\sigma}\qquad\text{(excess return per unit of volatility — the Sharpe ratio)}$$

and substitute:

$$dS_t=\mu S_t\,dt+\sigma S_t\big(dW^{\mathbb{Q}}_t-\theta\,dt\big) =\big(\mu-\sigma\theta\big)S_t\,dt+\sigma S_t\,dW^{\mathbb{Q}}_t =\;\underbrace{r S_t\,dt+\sigma S_t\,dW^{\mathbb{Q}}_t}_{\text{drift is now }r}$$

Only the drift moved: \(\mu\rightsquigarrow r\), with \(\sigma\) unchanged. \(Z_t\) is itself a martingale with \(Z_0=1\), verifiable with Itô in two lines, and the \(-\tfrac12\int\theta^2\) term exists precisely to cancel the Itô correction of the exponential.

Going deeper: why "equivalent" is exactly the right condition

\(\mathbb{P}\) and \(\mathbb{Q}\) are equivalent when they agree on which events have probability zero. Economically this is the no-arbitrage condition in disguise: if \(\mathbb{Q}\) could assign probability zero to an event \(\mathbb{P}\) thinks is possible, you could construct a payoff that is worthless under \(\mathbb{Q}\) (hence free) but pays out with positive real probability. Free money.

This also explains the hard boundary: you can change drift, never volatility. Two GBMs with different \(\sigma\) generate mutually singular measures — the \(\sigma=0.2\) world assigns probability zero to every path with realised vol 0.3, and vice versa. Since quadratic variation is observable from one path, the two worlds are distinguishable with certainty by an experiment lasting an instant. No equivalent measure change can bridge them. This is the precise reason stochastic volatility models are incomplete: there's a second risk factor you can't hedge or reweight away, so the martingale measure is no longer unique.

Visualize it

The measure-change visualizer, in a binomial setting where you can see every weight. The tree's shape — the set of reachable nodes — is fixed. Slide the market price of risk and watch the probability mass slosh from the \(\mathbb{P}\)-distribution (grey) to the \(\mathbb{Q}\)-distribution (teal). Every node that had positive mass still has positive mass. The mean moves; the support never does. Then check the readout: the option price computed under \(\mathbb{Q}\) is unchanged as you drag \(\mathbb{P}\)'s drift around — which is the punchline of the next section.

How this connects

Depends on

  • Itô's lemma — to verify \(Z\) and to do the substitution.
  • Quadratic variation — the invariant that bounds what Girsanov can do.

Feeds into

  • Risk-neutral valuation — Girsanov is the construction of \(\mathbb{Q}\); risk-neutral pricing is what you do with it.
  • Black–Scholes — the reason \(\mu\) is absent from the formula.
  • Numéraire changes: forward measure, stock measure — used to price rate and FX derivatives cleanly.
  • Importance sampling in Monte Carlo is Girsanov applied for variance reduction.

A useful compression: Girsanov is the theorem; the risk-neutral measure is the application; \(\theta=(\mu-r)/\sigma\) is the dial. Set \(\theta\) to the Sharpe ratio and you've removed exactly the compensation investors demand for bearing risk — leaving a world where every asset drifts at \(r\).

Common confusion

"Changing measure changes the stock price / the physical reality." It changes neither. The stock will do what the stock does; a path that happens, happens. What changes is the weighting you use when averaging over paths — and you're allowed to change it because the final answer (the price) is provably the same either way, thanks to hedging. A secondary version of the same error: believing \(\mathbb{Q}\) is a forecast. \(\mathbb{Q}\)-probabilities are not predictions of anything. If you use them for risk management or capital allocation, you will systematically underestimate expected returns, because you deliberately deleted the risk premium.

9. Risk-neutral valuation — why price is a discounted expectation under \(\mathbb{Q}\)

Why this matters

This is the central idea of derivative pricing and the one most likely to have been taught to you as a formula rather than an argument. The claim is not "investors are risk-neutral." The claim is far stranger and far stronger: if a payoff can be manufactured by trading, its price is forced, and the forced price happens to look like an expectation under a fictional measure.

The intuition

Forget options for a second. Suppose I offer to sell you a box that contains exactly one share of stock and 10 dollars in cash. What's the box worth? Obviously \(S+10\). Not "roughly", not "depending on your risk appetite" — exactly. If I quoted 5 dollars less, you'd buy the box, sell the share, pocket the difference, and walk away with certain profit. The box's price is pinned by the fact that you can replicate it.

The astonishing discovery of Black, Scholes and Merton is that an option is also a box — it's just that the recipe changes over time. A call option can be manufactured by holding \(\Delta_t\) shares and some cash, and continuously adjusting \(\Delta_t\) as the stock moves. If that replication works, the option's price is pinned by exactly the same argument as the box. Not because anybody is indifferent to risk, but because if two things have identical payoffs in every state of the world, they must have identical prices, or someone gets free money.

Now the sleight of hand. We have a price that's determined by replication cost. Why does it look like an expectation? Because of the martingale argument: if the discounted price of the hedge portfolio is a fair game under \(\mathbb{Q}\), and the hedge portfolio equals the option's payoff at expiry, then the option's discounted price must also be a fair game — and a fair game's current value is its expected future value. So

replication cost = discounted \(\mathbb{Q}\)-expectation of the payoff.

Two completely different-looking quantities, provably equal. The left side is what a trader does. The right side is what a quant computes.

And the reason \(\mu\) drops out: the hedger doesn't care where the stock goes. They're long the option and short \(\Delta\) shares; a move up gains on one leg and loses on the other. Their P&L depends only on how much the stock moved, not which direction — that is, only on \(\sigma\). Since the price is the hedger's cost and the hedger's cost is \(\mu\)-independent, \(\mu\) cannot appear in the price. The risk-neutral measure isn't an assumption imposed on the problem; it's the residue of a \(\mu\) that was never relevant.

Why "risk-neutral", then? Purely because in a hypothetical world of risk-indifferent investors, every asset would have to return \(r\) (nobody would demand compensation for risk) — and \(\mathbb{Q}\) is precisely the measure with that property. It's a naming convention describing a world we don't live in, and it has confused every generation of students since 1973.

The formal math

No arbitrage. There is no admissible self-financing strategy with \(V_0\le 0\), \(V_T\ge 0\) a.s. and \(\mathbb{P}(V_T>0)>0\).

First Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing. No arbitrage \(\iff\) there exists a measure \(\mathbb{Q}\sim\mathbb{P}\) under which every discounted tradeable price is a martingale:

$$\frac{S_t}{B_t}=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[\frac{S_T}{B_T}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_t\right], \qquad B_t=\exp\Big(\int_0^t r_s\,ds\Big).$$

Second FTAP. The market is complete (every payoff replicable) \(\iff\) \(\mathbb{Q}\) is unique. Under completeness, the price of any claim \(H\) at time \(t\) is

$$\boxed{\;V_t=B_t\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[\frac{H}{B_T}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_t\right] \;\overset{\text{const }r}{=}\;e^{-r(T-t)}\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\big[H\mid\mathcal{F}_t\big]\;}$$

Under \(\mathbb{Q}\), the stock's dynamics are \(dS=rS\,dt+\sigma S\,dW^{\mathbb{Q}}\), hence

$$S_T=S_t\exp\Big[\big(r-\tfrac12\sigma^2\big)(T-t)+\sigma\big(W^{\mathbb{Q}}_T-W^{\mathbb{Q}}_t\big)\Big], \qquad \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[S_T\mid\mathcal{F}_t]=S_te^{r(T-t)}.$$

Read the boxed formula as three instructions, in order: (1) pretend the stock drifts at \(r\); (2) average the payoff over that fictional world; (3) discount at \(r\). Steps 1 and 3 both use \(r\) but for unrelated reasons — one is a measure change, the other is the time value of money. Conflating them is a classic source of sign errors.

Going deeper: the numéraire idea — why the bank account isn't special

Nothing forces you to measure wealth in dollars-in-the-bank. Pick any strictly positive tradeable asset \(N\) as your unit of account (your numéraire), and there's a corresponding measure \(\mathbb{Q}^N\) under which every price divided by \(N\) is a martingale:

$$\frac{V_t}{N_t}=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}^N}\!\left[\frac{V_T}{N_T}\Big|\mathcal{F}_t\right]$$

Choosing well makes hard problems easy. Use a zero-coupon bond \(P(t,T)\) as numéraire and stochastic interest rates stop coupling to the payoff — that's the forward measure, and it's why rate derivatives are tractable at all. Use the stock itself and you get a slick derivation of the \(N(d_1)\) term in Black–Scholes: \(N(d_1)\) is the exercise probability under the stock measure, while \(N(d_2)\) is the exercise probability under \(\mathbb{Q}\). Those two numbers differ because they're computed in different currencies.

Visualize it

One period, two outcomes, complete transparency. Drag the real-world probability \(p\) anywhere you like — 5%, 95%, it doesn't matter — and watch the option price refuse to move. Then read the replicating portfolio underneath: the shares and cash you'd actually hold. That portfolio's cost is the price, and it never consulted \(p\). Finally, break the market: set \(u\), \(d\) and \(r\) so that \(q\) escapes \([0,1]\) and the panel turns red — that's an arbitrage, and it's exactly the configuration where no risk-neutral measure exists.

How this connects

Depends on

  • Martingales — the definition of \(\mathbb{Q}\).
  • Girsanov — the existence and construction of \(\mathbb{Q}\).
  • Expectation — the price is one.

Feeds into

  • Feynman–Kac — converts this expectation into a PDE.
  • Binomial model — the discrete instance you should build first.
  • Black–Scholes — evaluate the expectation for a lognormal \(S_T\) and you're done.
  • Put–call parity — pure no-arbitrage, no model needed.
  • Monte Carlo — simulate under \(\mathbb{Q}\), average, discount.

There are exactly two roads from here to Black–Scholes, and they arrive at the same place: the PDE road (hedge, apply Itô, eliminate risk, solve) and the probabilistic road (change measure, take an expectation, integrate). Feynman–Kac is the proof that they're the same road.

Common confusion

"Risk-neutral pricing assumes investors don't care about risk." The opposite is closer to true — the whole framework exists because investors do care, so we route around their preferences entirely rather than trying to model them. The technical statement: replication makes the price preference-free, so any valid pricing formula must be independent of risk aversion, and the risk-neutral form is simply the tidiest such formula. The real-world drift \(\mu\) still matters enormously — for whether you should own the stock, for VaR, for capital, for expected P&L. It just doesn't matter for what a hedgeable derivative on it costs.

10. Feynman–Kac — why Black–Scholes has both a PDE and a formula

Why this matters

You will be shown Black–Scholes twice: once as a partial differential equation, once as an integral against a normal density. Nobody tells you why the same model has two faces. Feynman–Kac is the answer, and it's one of the genuinely beautiful theorems in mathematics: diffusion and averaging are the same operation.

The intuition

Two ways to describe how heat spreads through a metal bar.

The physicist's way (PDE, local, deterministic). At each point, temperature changes at a rate proportional to the local curvature of the temperature profile: \(\partial_t u=\tfrac12\partial_{xx}u\). Hot spots are concave down, so they cool; cold spots are concave up, so they warm. This is a purely local rule, and it never mentions randomness.

The probabilist's way (expectation, global, random). Release a swarm of particles that random-walk around the bar. The temperature at point \(x\) after time \(t\) is the average initial temperature at the places those particles came from: \(u(t,x)=\mathbb{E}[u_0(x+W_t)]\).

These give identical answers. Always. That equivalence is Feynman–Kac, and once you see why, it stops being surprising: the second derivative in the PDE and the variance of the random walk are the same object. Quadratic variation is what connects them — the \(\tfrac12\partial_{xx}\) in the heat equation and the \(\tfrac12\sigma^2f''\) in Itô's lemma are literally the same term.

Translate that to finance. An option's value is an expectation over future stock paths — global, integral, probabilistic. It is also the solution to a local PDE describing how value bleeds from one instant to the next — local, differential, deterministic. Same object, two languages. And each language is better at different jobs:

  • The expectation handles high dimensions gracefully (Monte Carlo error doesn't care how many assets you have) and path-dependent payoffs naturally. It's terrible at early exercise.
  • The PDE handles early exercise beautifully (just check at every grid node whether exercising beats holding) and gives you all the Greeks for free as finite differences of the solution grid. It dies above three or four dimensions.

Knowing they're equivalent means you can derive in whichever language is convenient and compute in whichever language is efficient. That's not a philosophical nicety; it's how every derivatives desk actually operates.

One more piece of intuition, for the discounting. The general theorem allows a term \(-rV\) in the PDE, matching a factor \(e^{-r(T-t)}\) in the expectation. Probabilistically this is a killing rate: imagine each diffusing particle has a chance \(r\,dt\) of being annihilated in each instant. Fewer particles survive to carry value back to you — and "value decays exponentially at rate \(r\)" is exactly what discounting means. Discounting is particle death. Once you see that, the \(-rV\) term in Black–Scholes stops looking bolted on.

The formal math

Let \(X\) solve \(dX_t=\mu(t,X_t)\,dt+\sigma(t,X_t)\,dW_t\). Suppose \(v(t,x)\) solves the terminal-value problem

$$\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}+\mu(t,x)\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} +\frac12\sigma^2(t,x)\frac{\partial^2v}{\partial x^2}-r\,v=0,\qquad v(T,x)=h(x).$$

Then \(v\) has the probabilistic representation

$$\boxed{\;v(t,x)=\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{-r(T-t)}\,h(X_T)\;\Big|\;X_t=x\Big]\;}$$

Proof, in four lines; it uses nothing beyond Tier 1.

  1. Define \(M_s=e^{-r(s-t)}v(s,X_s)\), the discounted value process.
  2. Apply Itô: \(dM_s=e^{-r(s-t)}\big[\underbrace{v_t+\mu v_x+\tfrac12\sigma^2v_{xx}-rv}_{=\,0\text{ by the PDE}}\big]ds+e^{-r(s-t)}\sigma v_x\,dW_s\).
  3. The \(ds\) term vanishes because \(v\) solves the PDE, so \(M\) is a martingale.
  4. Therefore \(v(t,x)=M_t=\mathbb{E}[M_T\mid X_t=x]=\mathbb{E}[e^{-r(T-t)}h(X_T)\mid X_t=x]\). ∎

Look at what the proof actually used: Itô's lemma to expand, the PDE to kill the drift, and the martingale property to slide from \(t\) to \(T\). "PDE" and "zero drift" are the same statement. The differential operator in the PDE is the process's infinitesimal generator \(\mathcal{L}=\mu\partial_x+\tfrac12\sigma^2\partial_{xx}\), and the PDE reads \(\partial_t v+\mathcal{L}v=rv\).

Going deeper: forward vs backward — Kolmogorov's two equations

Feynman–Kac is a backward equation: it runs in \(t\) with a terminal condition and answers "what is this payoff worth from here?" Its variable is the starting point.

Its mirror is the Kolmogorov forward (Fokker–Planck) equation, which propagates the probability density \(p(T,y)\) forward from an initial condition:

$$\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\big[\mu\,p\big]+\frac12\frac{\partial^2}{\partial y^2}\big[\sigma^2p\big]$$

Both matter in practice, and they're the two halves of the same coin. The forward equation is what Dupire's local volatility formula is derived from — it lets you read the whole density off market option prices and then invert for \(\sigma(t,S)\). Backward: price one claim under many scenarios. Forward: find the distribution, then price everything.

This resolves why Black–Scholes has both a PDE and a closed form containing \(N(d_1)\): not because two independent derivations happen to exist, but because Feynman–Kac identifies a diffusion's conditional expectations with the solution of a PDE. The PDE and the expectation are one object; the \(N(d)\) terms are what the lognormal integral evaluates to.

Common confusion

Thinking Feynman–Kac is a numerical technique or an approximation. It's an exact identity between two objects. A related and subtler error: assuming it works for any PDE. It needs a parabolic, second-order PDE whose coefficients match a diffusion's drift and variance, with a terminal (not initial) condition, plus regularity/growth conditions for the stochastic integral to be a true martingale rather than a local one. Push it at an elliptic or hyperbolic equation and there's no diffusion to represent it.

11. The binomial tree — Black–Scholes with the calculus removed

Why this matters

Every conceptual ingredient of Black–Scholes — replication, no-arbitrage, the risk-neutral measure, the disappearance of \(\mu\), backward induction — appears in the binomial model using nothing beyond simultaneous equations. If Black–Scholes feels like magic, it's because you were shown it before this.

The intuition

Radical simplification: over the next period the stock does exactly one of two things. It goes up to \(uS\) or down to \(dS\). That's it. Two outcomes.

Now here is the trick, and it's the whole subject in miniature. I own a call option on this stock. I want to build a portfolio of shares and cash that pays exactly what the option pays — in both states. Two states, two unknowns (\(\Delta\) shares, \(B\) in the bank). Two equations, two unknowns. It just solves. There's no probability anywhere in that system of equations — I never asked how likely the up-move is, because I need the payoffs to match in both states regardless.

Solve it and \(\Delta=\dfrac{C_u-C_d}{uS-dS}\) — the rise-over-run of the option's payoff across the two states. That's delta, and here it's not a derivative, it's a difference quotient. Then the option's price is just what that portfolio costs today: \(C=\Delta S+B\). Done. No stochastic calculus, no measure theory, and \(p\) — the real probability of an up-move — never appeared.

Only after the fact do you notice that the answer can be rearranged into the form of an expectation, using a number \(q=\frac{e^{r\Delta t}-d}{u-d}\) that behaves like a probability. That \(q\) is the risk-neutral probability, and it's defined by being the weight that makes the discounted stock a martingale. Note the logical order: the risk-neutral measure is a consequence of the replication algebra, not an assumption supplied to it.

Extend to many periods by backward induction: fill in the payoffs at expiry, then walk backwards, at each node computing the discounted \(q\)-weighted average of its two children. The tree recombines (up-then-down = down-then-up) because the process is Markov, so \(n\) steps give \(n{+}1\) terminal nodes rather than \(2^n\).

Finally, shrink the time step. Choose \(u=e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}\) — note the \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\), which should now feel inevitable rather than arbitrary — and the tree's terminal distribution converges to a lognormal by the CLT, and the price converges to Black–Scholes. Black–Scholes is the binomial model in the limit. Two-state replication becomes continuous delta hedging; the discrete \(q\) becomes the measure \(\mathbb{Q}\); backward induction becomes the PDE.

The formal math

One period. Match the option payoff in both states:

$$\Delta\,uS+Be^{r\Delta t}=C_u,\qquad \Delta\,dS+Be^{r\Delta t}=C_d$$ $$\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta=\frac{C_u-C_d}{S(u-d)},\qquad B=e^{-r\Delta t}\frac{uC_d-dC_u}{u-d}$$

Price = cost of that portfolio, then rearranged into expectation form:

$$C=\Delta S+B=e^{-r\Delta t}\Big[q\,C_u+(1-q)\,C_d\Big],\qquad \boxed{q=\frac{e^{r\Delta t}-d}{u-d}}$$

Check that \(q\) is the martingale weight — it's the defining property, not a coincidence:

$$q\,uS+(1-q)\,dS=S\,e^{r\Delta t}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad e^{-r\Delta t}\mathbb{E}^q[S_1]=S_0$$

No-arbitrage condition. \(q\in(0,1)\) requires \(d

Multi-period, backward induction. With \(S_{ij}=S_0u^jd^{\,i-j}\) at step \(i\), node \(j\):

$$V_{n,j}=h(S_{n,j}),\qquad V_{i,j}=e^{-r\Delta t}\big[q\,V_{i+1,j+1}+(1-q)\,V_{i+1,j}\big]$$

For an American option, insert the early-exercise check at every node — trivial here, and the reason trees never died out:

$$V_{i,j}=\max\Big\{h(S_{i,j}),\;e^{-r\Delta t}\big[qV_{i+1,j+1}+(1-q)V_{i+1,j}\big]\Big\}$$

Cox–Ross–Rubinstein calibration — match the first two moments of log-returns per step:

$$u=e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}},\qquad d=1/u=e^{-\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}},\qquad \Delta t=T/n$$

Then \(\log S_T\) is a sum of \(n\) i.i.d. \(\pm\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\) steps with \(q\)-mean \((r-\tfrac12\sigma^2)\Delta t\); the CLT delivers \(\log S_T\sim\mathcal{N}\big((r-\tfrac12\sigma^2)T,\,\sigma^2T\big)\), which is exactly the \(\mathbb{Q}\)-distribution from the last tier. The \(-\tfrac12\sigma^2\) appears here for the same reason it appeared in Itô: it's Jensen, again.

Going deeper: why the convergence oscillates, and how desks fix it

Plot binomial price against \(n\) and you get a sawtooth around the Black–Scholes value rather than a smooth approach. The cause: whether a tree node lands exactly on the strike \(K\) flips as \(n\) increments, so the discretisation error alternates in sign. Convergence is \(O(1/n)\) but with an oscillating constant, which makes naive extrapolation unreliable.

Standard fixes: (a) average consecutive \(n\) — cancels the leading oscillation almost exactly; (b) Leisen–Reimer trees, which choose \(u,d,q\) so a node sits on the strike by construction, giving smooth \(O(1/n^2)\) convergence; (c) Richardson extrapolation on \(2n\) and \(n\). Depends on

  • Martingales — \(q\) is defined by the martingale condition.
  • Markov property — why the tree recombines.

Feeds into

The one-period binomial price already contains the full economic content of Black–Scholes. What remains is analysis, not economics.

Common confusion

"\(q\) is a probability, so the model assumes the stock goes up with probability \(q\)." No. \(q\) is a discounting weight that happens to lie in \([0,1]\) and therefore happens to be expressible as a probability. The real probability \(p\) exists, matters for whether you should buy the stock, and appears nowhere in the option price. In fact you can price the option without ever mentioning \(q\) at all — just compute \(\Delta S+B\). The expectation form is a convenient rewriting, and treating it as the primitive is what makes risk-neutral pricing feel like an unjustified assumption later.

12. Black–Scholes–Merton — replication, the PDE, and the formula

Why this matters

It is the reference point for the entire derivatives market — not because anyone believes it, but because it's the universal language. Every quote, every risk report, every "vol" number is stated in Black–Scholes units. You need to know exactly what it assumes so you know exactly where it breaks.

The intuition

Take the binomial hedging argument and run it continuously. I'm short one call, worth \(V(t,S)\). I hold \(\Delta\) shares against it. Over the next instant the stock moves; my option loses (or gains) and my shares gain (or lose). If I choose \(\Delta=\partial V/\partial S\), the two first-order moves cancel exactly. My portfolio's value no longer depends on which way the stock went.

But it isn't riskless yet, and this is where quadratic variation collects its debt. The option is a curved function of \(S\); my share position is linear. Cancelling the first-order terms leaves the second-order term standing — \(\tfrac12\Gamma(dS)^2=\tfrac12\Gamma\sigma^2S^2dt\) — and that term is not random. It's a known, deterministic bleed proportional to \(dt\). So my "risk-free" portfolio has a predictable drift, and no-arbitrage says that drift must equal \(r\) times the portfolio's value. Set those two equal and you have the Black–Scholes PDE. That is the entire derivation.

Notice what happened to \(\mu\): it multiplied \(dt\) in the stock's dynamics, but the hedge cancelled the \(dS\) term wholesale, taking \(\mu\) with it. The stock's expected return is deleted by the hedge, not assumed away. This is the concrete mechanism behind Girsanov.

To get the closed form, take the Feynman–Kac route instead: \(C=e^{-rT}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[(S_T-K)^+]\), with \(S_T\) lognormal under \(\mathbb{Q}\). That's a Gaussian integral over the region \(S_T>K\), and it separates into two pieces because the payoff has two pieces. Hence two terms, and hence two \(N(d)\)'s.

Reading the formula in words, which is the part usually omitted:

$$C=\underbrace{S_0}_{\text{buy the stock}}\underbrace{N(d_1)}_{\substack{\text{shares you actually}\\\text{need to hold}}}-\underbrace{Ke^{-rT}}_{\substack{\text{PV of the cash}\\\text{you'd pay}}}\underbrace{N(d_2)}_{\substack{\mathbb{Q}\text{-probability you}\\\text{end up paying it}}}$$

The second term is honest and easy: with probability \(N(d_2)\) you exercise and hand over \(K\), so you owe the present value of \(K\) times that probability. The first term is subtler: it is not "the stock times the probability of exercise". It's the expected value of the stock conditional on ending above \(K\), discounted — which is larger than \(S_0N(d_2)\) because if you exercise, you tend to be well above the strike, not just barely above. Equivalently: \(N(d_1)\) is the exercise probability computed under the stock numéraire rather than the cash numéraire. And it doubles as the hedge ratio, which is a genuine and useful coincidence: \(\Delta = N(d_1)\).

The formal math

Assumptions (each one is a future Tier 4 topic): GBM with constant \(\sigma\); constant \(r\); no dividends (easily relaxed); continuous, frictionless, costless trading; no arbitrage; unlimited shorting and divisibility.

Step 1 — Itô on the option. With \(dS=\mu S\,dt+\sigma S\,dW\):

$$dV=\Big(V_t+\mu SV_S+\tfrac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS}\Big)dt+\sigma SV_S\,dW$$

Step 2 — form the hedged portfolio \(\Pi=V-\Delta S\) with \(\Delta=V_S\):

$$d\Pi=dV-V_S\,dS=\Big(V_t+\tfrac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS}\Big)dt$$

Both the \(dW\) term and every \(\mu\) have vanished. What remains is deterministic — but only because \((dS)^2=\sigma^2S^2dt\) is deterministic.

Step 3 — no arbitrage forces \(d\Pi=r\Pi\,dt=r(V-SV_S)dt\). Equating:

$$\boxed{\;\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}+rS\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}+\frac12\sigma^2S^2\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial S^2}-rV=0\;}$$

with \(V(T,S)=\max(S-K,0)\) for a call. Compare it to Feynman–Kac: identical, with drift \(rS\) and diffusion \(\sigma S\) — i.e. the \(\mathbb{Q}\)-dynamics. The PDE is the risk-neutral expectation.

Step 4 — solve. Substituting \(x=\log S\), \(\tau=T-t\) turns it into the heat equation; or just do the lognormal integral. Either way:

$$C=S_0N(d_1)-Ke^{-rT}N(d_2),\qquad P=Ke^{-rT}N(-d_2)-S_0N(-d_1)$$ $$d_1=\frac{\ln(S_0/K)+\big(r+\tfrac12\sigma^2\big)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}},\qquad d_2=d_1-\sigma\sqrt{T}$$

Term by term: \(\ln(S_0/K)\) is moneyness in log space; \(rT\) is the forward drift; the \(\pm\tfrac12\sigma^2T\) is the Jensen/Itô convexity term from section 6, appearing with opposite signs in \(d_1\) and \(d_2\); and dividing by \(\sigma\sqrt{T}\) converts everything into standard deviations — so \(d_2\) is literally "how many \(\mathbb{Q}\)-sigmas is the strike below the forward".

Going deeper: the three assumptions that break first, in order of severity

1. Constant volatility. Comprehensively false; it's the reason for the volatility smile, and the market's response was to keep the formula and let \(\sigma\) vary by strike and maturity — which is logically incoherent (one stock, many vols) but operationally superb. Black–Scholes survives as a quoting convention: a bijection between price and one number.

2. Continuous paths. Markets gap. Overnight, on earnings, in crises. A gap is exactly the event delta hedging cannot protect against, because your hedge is calibrated to infinitesimal moves. See jump-diffusion.

3. Costless continuous trading. Rebalancing infinitely often at zero cost is impossible; with transaction costs, perfect replication costs infinitely much (Leland's result), and hedging becomes an optimisation with a no-trade band rather than an exact algorithm.

Worth noting what does not break it: the assumption that returns are lognormal is not needed for the hedging argument to be economically meaningful, only for the closed form. That's why the delta-hedging intuition survives into every Tier 4 model even when the formula doesn't.

The clean statement of what Black–Scholes achieved: it converted an economic question ("what compensation do I need for bearing this risk?") into an engineering question ("what does it cost to manufacture this payoff?"). Every later model keeps that reframing and changes only the manufacturing process.

Common confusion

"\(N(d_1)\) is the probability the option ends in the money." That's \(N(d_2)\). \(N(d_1)\) is both the delta and the in-the-money probability under the stock numéraire — a different measure, hence a different number. The gap between them is \(\sigma\sqrt{T}\), which is why they diverge for long-dated or high-vol options: at 40% vol over four years, \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) differ by 0.8 standard deviations, and confusing them is a large pricing error rather than a rounding one.

13. The Greeks — what a hedger actually feels

Why this matters

Nobody on a trading desk holds an option and waits. They hold a risk profile and manage it. The Greeks are the coordinates of that profile, and every one of them corresponds to a specific, physical thing that can go wrong overnight.

The intuition

Think of your option position as a vehicle and the Greeks as its dashboard. They are partial derivatives, yes — but each answers a concrete operational question.

GreekDerivativeThe hedger's question it answers
Delta \(\Delta\)\(\partial V/\partial S\) "How many shares do I need right now?" Your equivalent stock position. Delta 0.6 on 100 contracts = long 6,000 shares.
Gamma \(\Gamma\)\(\partial^2V/\partial S^2\) "How fast does my hedge go stale?" High gamma = you must re-trade constantly. It's the curvature that delta hedging cannot capture.
Vega \(\nu\)\(\partial V/\partial\sigma\) "What if the market's opinion about future turbulence changes?" Not a real Greek letter, and not a real derivative either — \(\sigma\) is supposed to be constant.
Theta \(\Theta\)\(\partial V/\partial t\) "What do I lose by doing nothing overnight?" The rent you pay for owning convexity.
Rho \(\rho\)\(\partial V/\partial r\) "What if funding costs move?" Usually the least of your worries — until it isn't.

The relationship that actually matters is gamma versus theta, and it's not a coincidence — it's the Black–Scholes PDE rearranged. If you're long an option and delta-hedged, then every time the stock moves you make money: you're long convexity, so you gain on both up-moves and down-moves (buy low, sell high, mechanically, as you rebalance). That's gamma, and it's a free lunch — except it isn't, because you pay for it continuously through theta. The option decays.

Long gamma = you get paid when the stock moves, and you pay rent every day it doesn't.

Being short gamma is the mirror image and it's how people blow up: you collect steady premium day after day, and one gap move takes back a year of it. The PDE says the trade is exactly fair if realised volatility equals the volatility you priced with. All of option trading is a bet on which side of that equality you're on.

A caveat on vega, since it is formally inconsistent: Black–Scholes assumes \(\sigma\) is a constant, so differentiating with respect to it is differentiating with respect to something the model says cannot change. It works because it's a sensitivity to a quoting parameter, not to a model parameter. Taking vega seriously as a hedge instrument is one of the motivations for stochastic volatility.

The formal math

For a non-dividend-paying call, with \(\varphi\) the standard normal density:

$$\Delta_C=N(d_1),\qquad \Delta_P=N(d_1)-1,\qquad \Gamma=\frac{\varphi(d_1)}{S\sigma\sqrt{T}}\ \ \text{(same for call and put)}$$ $$\nu=S\varphi(d_1)\sqrt{T}\ \ \text{(same for call and put)},\qquad \rho_C=KTe^{-rT}N(d_2),\qquad \rho_P=-KTe^{-rT}N(-d_2)$$ $$\Theta_C=-\frac{S\varphi(d_1)\sigma}{2\sqrt{T}}-rKe^{-rT}N(d_2)$$

The identity to remember. Substitute \(\Delta,\Gamma,\Theta\) into the Black–Scholes PDE and it reads:

$$\Theta+rS\Delta+\tfrac12\sigma^2S^2\Gamma=rV$$

For a delta-hedged portfolio (\(\Delta\) neutralised, \(V\approx0\) net of hedge) this collapses to

$$\Theta\approx-\tfrac12\sigma^2S^2\Gamma$$

There it is: gamma and theta are the same quantity with opposite signs. You cannot buy convexity without paying decay, and the exchange rate is fixed by \(\sigma^2\). This single line is the most useful thing in the section — it's why "is implied vol too high or too low?" is the only question an options trader is really asking.

Going deeper: the discrete hedging P&L equation

Hedge a short option at implied vol \(\sigma_{\text{imp}}\) while the stock actually realises \(\sigma_{\text{real}}\). Over one rebalancing interval, the P&L is

$$\delta\Pi\;\approx\;\tfrac12\,\Gamma\,S^2\Big[\big(\tfrac{\delta S}{S}\big)^2-\sigma_{\text{imp}}^2\,\delta t\Big]$$

Summing over the life of the trade, total P&L \(\approx\tfrac12\int\Gamma S^2(\sigma_{\text{real}}^2-\sigma_{\text{imp}}^2)dt\). You are literally long realised quadratic variation and short implied. Three consequences that surprise people:

  • You can be right about direction and lose, or wrong and win — direction was hedged away.
  • The P&L is path-dependent even though the payoff isn't: the \(\Gamma S^2\) weight means moves that happen while you're near the strike count enormously more than moves that happen far away. Same terminal price, different P&L.
  • Variance swaps were invented precisely to strip out that \(\Gamma S^2\) weighting and give clean exposure to \(\int\sigma^2dt\).

How this connects

Depends on

  • Black–Scholes — these are its partial derivatives.
  • Quadratic variation — gamma P&L is realised variance.
  • Binomial delta — the same object, discretised.

Feeds into

  • Skew — vanna and volga (cross-Greeks in \(S\) and \(\sigma\)) are how desks price the smile's dynamics.
  • Jump risk — the Greek framework fails for gaps, which is exactly the point.
  • Portfolio risk management: books are aggregated and reported entirely in Greeks.

Practical mental model: delta is a position, gamma is a workload, theta is a bill, vega is an opinion.

Common confusion

"I'm delta-hedged, so I have no risk." Delta hedging removes only first-order exposure to small stock moves. You remain fully exposed to: the stock moving a lot (gamma), implied vol changing (vega), time passing (theta), and the stock gapping through your hedge (jump risk, which no Greek captures). Delta-neutral means "immune to the next tick", not "safe". Almost every famous derivatives loss involves a book that was delta-neutral and short gamma.

14. Put–call parity — the one relationship that needs no model

Why this matters

Everything else in Tier 3 assumed a model. This doesn't. Put–call parity holds under any dynamics whatsoever — jumps, stochastic vol, regime switches, whatever — because it follows from a static portfolio that requires no trading at all. It's the cleanest example in the subject of what pure no-arbitrage buys you.

The intuition

Build two portfolios and hold them to expiry, doing nothing.

  • A: one call struck at \(K\), plus \(Ke^{-rT}\) in cash.
  • B: one put struck at \(K\), plus one share.

At expiry the cash has grown to exactly \(K\). If \(S_T>K\): A's call is worth \(S_T-K\) and the cash is \(K\), total \(S_T\). B's put expires worthless and the share is worth \(S_T\), total \(S_T\). If \(S_T<K\): A's call is worthless, cash is \(K\), total \(K\). B's put is worth \(K-S_T\), share is \(S_T\), total \(K\). Identical in every state. Therefore identical today, or you have a money machine.

The deeper reading: a call and a put on the same strike differ by exactly a forward contract. Options are not two separate instruments — there is only one option, and the call/put labelling is a choice of how much stock exposure to bundle with it. That's why calls and puts share the same gamma, the same vega, and the same implied volatility. If a "put skew" existed but no corresponding "call skew", parity would be violated and you could arbitrage it. Every volatility smile is automatically consistent across calls and puts for this reason, and any dataset that isn't has stale quotes in it.

This is also the workhorse sanity check. Any pricing model, any implementation, any market data feed: check parity first. If it fails, something is broken, and you know that before investigating the model.

The formal math

European options, same \(K\) and \(T\), no dividends:

$$\boxed{\;C-P=S_0-Ke^{-rT}\;}$$

The underlying payoff identity, an algebraic fact about the max function:

$$\max(S_T-K,0)-\max(K-S_T,0)=S_T-K\qquad\text{for all }S_T.$$

With continuous dividend yield \(q\), and for a forward with delivery price \(K\):

$$C-P=S_0e^{-qT}-Ke^{-rT},\qquad F_0=S_0e^{(r-q)T}\ \Longrightarrow\ C-P=e^{-rT}(F_0-K).$$

Immediate corollaries you can verify against the Black–Scholes formulas above:

$$\Delta_C-\Delta_P=1\ \ (\text{since }N(d_1)-(N(d_1)-1)=1),\qquad \Gamma_C=\Gamma_P,\qquad \nu_C=\nu_P.$$

Those Greek identities are forced by parity, not by the Gaussian: the difference \(C-P\) is a linear function of \(S\), so it has zero second derivative and zero \(\sigma\)-sensitivity in any model. That's why they survive into Tier 4 unchanged.

Going deeper: why American options only get an inequality

The static-portfolio argument needs both options to be exercised at the same moment. For American options they might not be, and parity degrades to bounds:

$$S_0-K\;\le\;C_{\text{Am}}-P_{\text{Am}}\;\le\;S_0-Ke^{-rT}$$

Related classic result: an American call on a non-dividend-paying stock is never optimally exercised early, so it equals its European counterpart. Exercising early throws away the remaining time value and pays \(K\) sooner than necessary — strictly worse than selling the option. American puts, however, genuinely can be worth exercising early: receiving \(K\) now and earning interest on it can beat holding, particularly deep in the money at high rates. That asymmetry is why the tree widget shows a nonzero early-exercise premium for puts and essentially none for calls.

Note the hierarchy this reveals. Some results are model-free (parity, monotonicity in \(K\), convexity in \(K\)) and hold no matter what. Others are model-dependent (any actual price). Knowing which is which tells you which of your results can be broken by a bad model assumption and which can't.

Common confusion

"Put–call parity is a Black–Scholes result." It predates it and doesn't depend on it. Parity requires only that you can hold a share, hold cash, and not be arbitraged. It survives jumps, stochastic vol, fat tails, and crashes intact. Conversely — and this is the useful corollary — if you ever observe parity failing in real quotes, you have not found free money; you have found a dividend you forgot, a hard-to-borrow stock with negative repo, an early-exercise feature, or a stale price.

Beyond Black–Scholes

Each model below relaxes exactly one Black–Scholes assumption that fails empirically. They are not competing alternatives but successive repairs, and each is stated in terms of the specific failure it addresses.

15. The volatility smile — the evidence that Black–Scholes is wrong

Why this matters

The problem it fixes: Black–Scholes says one stock has one volatility. The market says otherwise, loudly, every day, in a pattern that has been stable for forty years. The smile is not a bug in the data — it's the market pricing something the model can't see.

The intuition

Here's the experiment that generates the smile. Take every listed option on one stock, one expiry. For each, take its market price and ask: what \(\sigma\) would I have to plug into Black–Scholes to reproduce this price? That number is the implied volatility. Since vega is strictly positive, price and implied vol are in one-to-one correspondence — quoting one is quoting the other, and traders quote vol because it's comparable across strikes and dates in a way that dollar prices aren't.

If Black–Scholes were correct, every strike would return the same number, and you'd get a flat line. You never do. Equity index options give a downward skew — low strikes imply much higher vol than high strikes. FX gives a symmetric smile. Both shapes have persisted since 1987, which was not a coincidence: the smile essentially didn't exist before Black Monday and has never gone away since.

Two distinct effects are embedded in this, and they are worth separating:

  • The real distribution has fat tails and is left-skewed. Big down-moves happen more often than a lognormal allows. A deep out-of-the-money put pays off exactly in those scenarios, so it's worth more than lognormal maths says — and expressing "worth more" in the language of a model with only one dial forces that dial up. High implied vol on low strikes is a fat left tail in disguise.
  • Crash risk carries a premium. Beyond the true probability, investors will overpay for protection that pays off precisely when everything else they own is collapsing. The \(\mathbb{Q}\)-distribution therefore has an even fatter left tail than the \(\mathbb{P}\)-one. The smile mixes real tail probability with risk aversion, and no amount of statistics separates them from option prices alone.

Why is equity skewed rather than symmetric? Leverage: as a firm's equity falls, its debt-to-equity ratio rises and the equity mechanically becomes more volatile. Plus the plain empirical fact that markets fall faster than they rise — panics are correlated, rallies are gradual.

Now the philosophically uncomfortable part, which you should confront rather than let pass: quoting a different \(\sigma\) per strike is internally inconsistent. A single stock cannot simultaneously have volatility 0.28 and 0.19. The market does it anyway because Black–Scholes has been demoted from a model to a unit of account — a bijection between price and one interpretable number. All of Tier 4's remaining models exist to build something that generates a smile from consistent dynamics instead of papering over it.

The formal math

Implied volatility \(\sigma_{\text{imp}}(K,T)\) is defined implicitly by inverting the pricing formula against the observed price:

$$C^{\text{BS}}\big(S,K,T,r,\sigma_{\text{imp}}(K,T)\big)=C^{\text{mkt}}(K,T)$$

Well-posed because \(\partial C/\partial\sigma=\nu>0\) — strictly monotone, so the root is unique and Newton's method converges fast.

Breeden–Litzenberger — the smile is the risk-neutral density, recoverable exactly:

$$q_T(K)=e^{rT}\frac{\partial^2C}{\partial K^2}\bigg|_{K}$$

A remarkable result: from a continuum of call prices you can read off the entire \(\mathbb{Q}\)-density of \(S_T\) with no model at all. The smile is not "wrong volatility", it is a completely accurate report of a non-lognormal density expressed in the wrong coordinates.

Two model-free no-arbitrage constraints the surface must respect, worth knowing because interpolating a surface carelessly violates them:

$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial K}\in[-e^{-rT},0]\ \ \text{(monotone)},\qquad \frac{\partial^2C}{\partial K^2}\ge0\ \ \text{(convex — else negative density)}$$

Common parameterisation of a slice (SVI), in log-moneyness \(k=\log(K/F)\):

$$w(k)=\sigma_{\text{imp}}^2T=a+b\Big(\rho(k-m)+\sqrt{(k-m)^2+s^2}\Big)$$
Going deeper: term structure, and why the surface is a surface

Skew flattens with maturity, roughly like \(1/\sqrt{T}\). The reason is the CLT: a one-day return can be wildly non-Gaussian, but a two-year return is the sum of 500 daily returns and Gaussian-ness reasserts itself. So the fat tails that drive skew are a short-horizon phenomenon.

This creates a real modelling difficulty that motivates the next section. Pure jump models produce steep short-dated skew (good) that flattens too fast (bad). Pure stochastic vol produces good long-dated skew but too little at the short end, because vol needs time to diffuse anywhere interesting. Fitting the whole surface essentially forces you to combine both — hence models like Bates (Heston + jumps).

Depends on

  • Vega — its positivity is what makes implied vol well-defined.
  • Put–call parity — guarantees one surface serves both.

Feeds into

  • Stochastic vol — built to generate a smile from dynamics.
  • Jump-diffusion — built to generate short-dated skew.
  • Exotic pricing, where getting the smile wrong is the dominant source of error.

The smile is the hinge of the whole tier: it's simultaneously the evidence that Black–Scholes fails, the calibration target every replacement must hit, and the reason the replacements exist.

Common confusion

"Implied vol is the market's forecast of future volatility." Only partly, and the gap is systematic. Implied vol is a \(\mathbb{Q}\)-quantity: it contains the true expected volatility plus a variance risk premium, because people pay up for protection. Empirically implied vol exceeds subsequent realised vol most of the time — which is why systematically selling options has positive average P&L and occasionally catastrophic P&L. Reading implied vol as a forecast will lead you to conclude the market is permanently, stupidly wrong. It isn't; you're reading a price as if it were a prediction.

16. Local and stochastic volatility — giving vol its own randomness

Why this matters

The problem it fixes: the smile exists and you need a model that produces it from coherent dynamics — so that you can price exotics and, crucially, so that you know how the smile will move when the spot moves. A model that fits today's prices but predicts the wrong smile dynamics will hedge badly no matter how well it calibrates.

The intuition

Two philosophically different repairs.

Local volatility (Dupire). Keep one source of randomness, but let volatility be a deterministic function of where and when you are: \(\sigma(t,S)\). It's the minimal change — the model stays complete, hedging stays exact, and Dupire's formula gives you \(\sigma(t,S)\) in closed form from the observed surface. Astonishingly, it fits every option price exactly, by construction.

So why isn't this the end of the story? Because a perfect fit today can still be a bad model of tomorrow. Local vol implies that when the spot falls, the whole smile slides with it in a very specific way — and empirically it slides about twice as far as the market's actual smile does. Since your delta depends on how you think the smile moves, local vol produces systematically wrong deltas for skew-sensitive products. It's a superb interpolator and a poor forecaster. Useful slogan: local vol fits the smile; it doesn't explain it.

Stochastic volatility (Heston). Admit the real cause: volatility is itself random and unpredictable. Give it its own SDE and its own Brownian motion. Volatility clusters (calm follows calm, chaos follows chaos), mean-reverts (a VIX of 80 does not persist for a year), and — critically — is negatively correlated with returns: markets fall and vol spikes together.

That correlation \(\rho\) is the workhorse parameter and it maps directly onto the smile's shape. Negative \(\rho\) means down-moves arrive with vol expansion, which fattens the left tail and tilts the smile into a skew. Meanwhile \(\xi\) (vol-of-vol) controls how curved the smile is — it fattens both tails symmetrically. So you have two dials with two distinct visual effects: \(\rho\) tilts, \(\xi\) bends. Once you see that, calibrating Heston stops being a black box.

The cost of this is structural: a second source of randomness means the market is incomplete. You cannot hedge volatility risk with the stock alone — there aren't enough instruments. From the second FTAP that means \(\mathbb{Q}\) is no longer unique; there's a whole family of arbitrage-free prices, and the market picks one via the market price of volatility risk. In practice you restore completeness by hedging with another option, which is why vega hedging is a real activity performed with real instruments rather than a theoretical afterthought.

The formal math

Local volatility. \(dS_t=rS_t\,dt+\sigma_{\text{loc}}(t,S_t)S_t\,dW_t\), with Dupire's formula recovering \(\sigma_{\text{loc}}\) from the call surface \(C(K,T)\):

$$\sigma_{\text{loc}}^2(T,K)=\frac{\dfrac{\partial C}{\partial T}+rK\dfrac{\partial C}{\partial K}} {\tfrac12K^2\dfrac{\partial^2C}{\partial K^2}}$$

Derived from the Kolmogorov forward equation — the mirror of Feynman–Kac mentioned in that section's deeper box. Note the denominator is the Breeden–Litzenberger density: local vol is a ratio of a time-derivative to a density, which is why it's so numerically delicate in the wings where the density is tiny.

Heston. Two coupled SDEs, with variance \(v_t\) following a CIR process:

$$dS_t=rS_t\,dt+\sqrt{v_t}\,S_t\,dW^S_t$$ $$dv_t=\underbrace{\kappa(\theta-v_t)}_{\text{mean reversion}}dt+\underbrace{\xi\sqrt{v_t}}_{\text{vol of vol}}dW^v_t, \qquad d\langle W^S,W^v\rangle_t=\rho\,dt$$
ParameterMeaningEffect on the smile
\(\theta\)long-run varianceoverall level
\(\kappa\)reversion speedhow fast the term structure flattens
\(\xi\)vol of volcurvature — bends both wings up
\(\rho\)spot/vol correlationtilt — \(\rho<0\) gives equity skew

The \(\sqrt{v_t}\) keeps variance non-negative; the Feller condition \(2\kappa\theta>\xi^2\) keeps it strictly positive. Heston's fame rests on having a semi-closed-form solution via the characteristic function:

$$C=S_0P_1-Ke^{-rT}P_2,\qquad P_j=\frac12+\frac1\pi\int_0^\infty\mathrm{Re}\!\left[\frac{e^{-iu\ln K}\varphi_j(u)}{iu}\right]du$$

Compare that to Black–Scholes: identical shape, with \(N(d_1),N(d_2)\) replaced by \(P_1,P_2\). Same two-term structure, same numéraire interpretation — one integral is the exercise probability under the stock measure, the other under \(\mathbb{Q}\). Recognising the skeleton makes the Fourier machinery far less alien.

Going deeper: SABR, and why practitioners often use neither pure model

SABR \(dF=\alpha F^\beta dW_1,\ d\alpha=\nu\alpha\,dW_2,\ \langle dW_1,dW_2\rangle=\rho\,dt\) dominates rates markets, not because it's more realistic but because Hagan's asymptotic expansion gives implied vol as an explicit algebraic formula. Calibrating one smile takes microseconds instead of an optimisation loop, and when you must recalibrate thousands of swaption smiles in a risk run, that difference decides the architecture.

Local-stochastic volatility (LSV) is what large desks actually run: a Heston-type engine multiplied by a local-vol "leverage function" calibrated so the model reprices vanillas exactly. You get local vol's perfect fit and stochastic vol's realistic dynamics. The calibration requires solving a nonlinear Fokker–Planck equation, which is why it took until the 2000s to become standard.

The general lesson, which recurs across quantitative finance: choose the model by what you're hedging and how fast you need it, not by realism alone.

  • Feynman–Kac — the pricing PDE is now two-dimensional.

This continues the line opened in Tier 0: the Markov property is a statement about the chosen state variable, not about the world. Every model in Tier 4 enlarges the state until the Markov property is recovered.

Common confusion

"Local vol and stochastic vol are two approximations of the same thing." They are structurally different objects. Local vol has one Brownian motion and is complete — perfect hedging still exists. Stochastic vol has two and is incomplete — perfect hedging with the stock alone is impossible, and prices are no longer unique. And the relationship between them is precise, not vague: Gyöngy's theorem says the local vol implied by a stochastic vol model is \(\sigma_{\text{loc}}^2(t,K)=\mathbb{E}[v_t\mid S_t=K]\) — local vol is the conditional average of the stochastic variance. They match on vanillas by construction and diverge on anything path-dependent, which is exactly where the money is.

17. Jump-diffusion — crashes are not large diffusions

Why this matters

The problem it fixes: Black–Scholes paths are continuous, so a stock can only reach −20% by passing through −19%, −18%… and you can rehedge on the way. Real markets gap. October 1987 was a −22% day, which under a lognormal at 20% vol is roughly a 20-sigma event — something that should not have occurred in the lifetime of the universe. The model isn't slightly off in the tails; it's wrong about the mechanism.

The intuition

Merton's fix is honest and structurally simple: keep the diffusion for ordinary trading noise, and add a separate process for news. Most of the time the stock diffuses. Occasionally — at random times generated by a Poisson clock — something happens, and the price jumps discontinuously.

Two independent sources with different characters: continuous noise for the grind, jumps for the events. This immediately produces the empirical features Black–Scholes misses: fat tails (jumps put mass far from the centre), left skew (make the average jump negative), and short-dated skew that's steep. That last point is the real selling point. Over one day, a diffusion basically can't move 15%, so a diffusion model prices a one-week 15%-OTM put at nearly zero. A jump model says: it's not about diffusing there, it's about whether the clock rings. Jump risk barely scales with horizon, which is exactly the short-dated skew the market shows and stochastic vol struggles to produce.

Now the consequence that matters more than the pricing formula. Jumps destroy replication. The delta-hedging argument assumed you could rebalance as the stock moved through every intermediate price. If the stock teleports from 100 to 78, your hedge was set for 100 and you take the loss with no opportunity to adjust. And you can't hedge jump risk with the stock, because one stock position cannot cover a continuum of possible jump sizes. The market is incomplete again, for a different reason than stochastic vol.

Merton's response was pragmatic: he assumed jump risk is diversifiable — idiosyncratic, unpriced, zero risk premium — which restores a unique price as a Poisson-weighted sum of Black–Scholes prices. Elegant, and clearly false for index options, where jumps are precisely the systematic, undiversifiable, everybody-loses-together risk that people pay most to avoid. That gap between Merton's assumption and reality is a large part of why index skew is as steep as it is.

The formal math

Merton's jump-diffusion, with \(N_t\) a Poisson process of intensity \(\lambda\) (expected jumps per year) and i.i.d. lognormal jump sizes \(Y_i\), \(\log Y\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_J,\sigma_J^2)\):

$$\frac{dS_t}{S_{t^-}}=(r-\lambda k)\,dt+\sigma\,dW_t+\big(Y_{N_t}-1\big)\,dN_t,\qquad k=\mathbb{E}[Y]-1=e^{\mu_J+\sigma_J^2/2}-1$$

The \(-\lambda k\,dt\) is a compensator: jumps have nonzero mean, so the continuous drift is reduced to keep \(\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[S_T]=S_0e^{rT}\). The martingale condition must still hold, and this term is what enforces it. Note \(S_{t^-}\) — the value just before the jump, which is the same left-endpoint / no-peeking convention as the Itô integral.

Itô's lemma gains a jump term (the continuous part is unchanged):

$$df=\Big(f_t+\mu Sf_S+\tfrac12\sigma^2S^2f_{SS}\Big)dt+\sigma Sf_S\,dW +\underbrace{\big[f(S_{t^-}Y)-f(S_{t^-})\big]dN_t}_{\text{finite, non-local}}$$

The jump term is a difference, not a derivative — no Taylor expansion, because the move isn't small. Consequently the pricing equation is a partial integro-differential equation (PIDE): value at \(S\) depends on value at every \(SY\).

Merton's closed form, under the diversifiable-jump assumption — condition on the number of jumps and each branch is Black–Scholes:

$$C=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\lambda'T}(\lambda'T)^n}{n!}\;C^{\text{BS}}\big(S,K,T,r_n,\sigma_n\big)$$ $$\sigma_n^2=\sigma^2+\frac{n\sigma_J^2}{T},\qquad r_n=r-\lambda k+\frac{n\ln(1+k)}{T},\qquad \lambda'=\lambda(1+k)$$

Beautifully interpretable: "given exactly \(n\) jumps happen, the world is lognormal with inflated variance — so price it with Black–Scholes and average over how many jumps occur." Conditioning on the Poisson count restores normality.

Contrast with stochastic vol: both create incompleteness, but for structurally different reasons — SV has an unhedgeable second diffusive factor, jumps have unhedgeable discontinuities. And they fix different maturities: jumps for the short end, SV for the long end. That complementarity is why production models use both.

Common confusion

"Just raise volatility to account for crashes." This is the single most common risk-management error and it fails in both directions. Raising \(\sigma\) inflates the price of every option including at-the-money ones, whereas jumps mostly affect the wings — so you overpay in the middle and still underprice the tails. And it produces a flat smile, when the problem you were trying to solve was a non-flat smile. Variance and tail risk are different quantities: one is the second moment, the other lives in the fourth moment and beyond. No setting of a single Gaussian's width reproduces a fat tail.

18. Short-rate models — when \(r\) stops being a constant

Why this matters

The problem it fixes: Black–Scholes treats \(r\) as a known constant. For a three-month equity option, fine. For a ten-year swaption, the interest rate is the underlying, and assuming it's constant prices the instrument at zero. Rates also behave qualitatively differently from stocks — they mean-revert, they're bounded in practice, and the whole yield curve moves at once.

The intuition

A stock has no natural level; if it doubles, the new price is as good a starting point as the old one. That's what geometric Brownian motion encodes, and it's why GBM is right for equities.

Interest rates are not like that. If the short rate hits 15%, the central bank and the economy push back; if it hits 0%, there's a floor of sorts and pressure upward. Rates live in a range and get pulled toward a level. The mathematical encoding of "pulled toward a level" is a drift proportional to the distance from it: \(\kappa(\theta-r_t)\). Far above \(\theta\), the drift is strongly negative; far below, strongly positive; at \(\theta\), zero. This is mean reversion, and it's the same Ornstein–Uhlenbeck structure that appears in Heston's variance process — for the same economic reason, since volatility also mean-reverts.

The parameter \(\kappa\) is best understood through its half-life: \(\ln2/\kappa\) is how long a shock takes to decay halfway. \(\kappa=0.5\) gives about 1.4 years, which is roughly what rates data suggests. And mean reversion has a direct market consequence: it damps long-rate volatility relative to short-rate volatility, which is why the yield curve's long end is much calmer than the front end.

Three model generations, each fixing the previous one's flaw:

  • Vasicek (1977). OU process. Everything is Gaussian, so bond prices are closed-form and beautiful. Flaw: Gaussian means rates can go negative — for decades considered a fatal embarrassment, and then in 2014 European rates went negative and this became a feature.
  • Cox–Ingersoll–Ross (1985). Multiply the volatility by \(\sqrt{r_t}\): as \(r\to0\) the randomness switches off and the positive drift lifts it away from zero. Rates stay positive (given Feller \(2\kappa\theta>\sigma^2\)), and volatility rises with the level of rates, which matches data. Still closed-form, via a non-central chi-square.
  • Heath–Jarrow–Morton (1992). A change of philosophy rather than a new SDE. Vasicek and CIR have a handful of parameters and therefore cannot reproduce today's actual yield curve — they predict a curve, and it's wrong, which is unacceptable when the curve is directly observable. HJM says: stop modelling the short rate and modelling the curve as an output. Model the evolution of the entire forward curve and take today's observed curve as the initial condition. Fit by construction.

HJM's famous result is that once you specify the forward-rate volatility structure, the drift is completely determined by no-arbitrage — you don't get to choose it. That's the martingale condition from Tier 1, reappearing as a constraint on an infinite-dimensional object.

The formal math

Vasicek: \(dr_t=\kappa(\theta-r_t)\,dt+\sigma\,dW_t\), with explicit solution

$$r_t=r_0e^{-\kappa t}+\theta(1-e^{-\kappa t})+\sigma\int_0^te^{-\kappa(t-s)}dW_s \;\sim\;\mathcal{N}\Big(r_0e^{-\kappa t}+\theta(1-e^{-\kappa t}),\ \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}(1-e^{-2\kappa t})\Big)$$

Note the stationary variance \(\sigma^2/2\kappa\) — unlike Brownian motion, the variance does not grow without bound. Mean reversion caps long-run uncertainty, and that cap is the mathematical reason 30-year rates are less volatile than 3-month rates.

CIR: \(dr_t=\kappa(\theta-r_t)\,dt+\sigma\sqrt{r_t}\,dW_t\), with Feller condition \(2\kappa\theta>\sigma^2\) ensuring \(r_t>0\).

Bond prices come straight from Feynman–Kac — the zero-coupon bond is a derivative whose payoff is 1 and whose state variable is \(r\):

$$P(t,T)=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[\exp\Big(-\int_t^Tr_s\,ds\Big)\bigg|\,\mathcal{F}_t\right] =A(t,T)\,e^{-B(t,T)\,r_t}$$

an affine term structure, with (Vasicek) \(B(t,T)=\frac{1-e^{-\kappa(T-t)}}{\kappa}\). The corresponding PDE is Feynman–Kac with \(r\) as the state:

$$\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}+\kappa(\theta-r)\frac{\partial P}{\partial r}+\frac12\sigma^2\frac{\partial^2P}{\partial r^2}-rP=0,\qquad P(T,T)=1.$$

HJM. Model instantaneous forwards \(f(t,T)\) with \(df(t,T)=\alpha(t,T)dt+\sigma(t,T)dW_t\). No-arbitrage then forces:

$$\boxed{\;\alpha(t,T)=\sigma(t,T)\int_t^T\sigma(t,u)\,du\;}$$

One free function (volatility), zero free drift. This is the same drift-is-not-yours-to-choose principle as Girsanov, scaled up to a curve. Choose \(\sigma(t,T)=\sigma e^{-\kappa(T-t)}\) and HJM reduces exactly to Hull–White (Vasicek with time-dependent \(\theta\)) — the generations are nested, not rival.

Depends on

  • Feynman–Kac — bond prices are its direct application.
  • Risk-neutral valuation — plus numéraire changes (the forward measure) to handle stochastic discounting.
  • Markov — again, state expansion: \(r\) joins the state.

  • Credit: intensity models are CIR processes for the default hazard rate.
  • Multi-curve frameworks, xVA, and every long-dated valuation.
  • Numerical methods — HJM is non-Markov in general and usually needs Monte Carlo.

Structurally: in equity derivatives, discounting is a nuisance parameter. In rates, discounting and the payoff are driven by the same random variable, so they can't be separated — which is precisely why the numéraire-change technology from Tier 2 was developed.

Common confusion

"Mean reversion means rates are predictable, so I can trade it." Mean reversion is a statement about the drift under \(\mathbb{P}\). Prices already embed the market's view of it: if everyone knows rates revert to 3%, forward rates reflect that, and betting on reversion is betting your \(\kappa\) and \(\theta\) beat the market's. A second, sharper version of the error: mean reversion under \(\mathbb{P}\) does not imply mean reversion under \(\mathbb{Q}\), and the difference is the term premium. Calibrating \(\kappa\) to historical rate data and then using it to price swaptions mixes the two measures and gives systematically wrong prices.

19. Monte Carlo and finite differences — what to reach for when there's no formula

Why this matters

The problem it fixes: closed forms exist for a handful of textbook cases. Real books contain American exercise, barriers, baskets, callable structures and stochastic-vol exotics, none of which have formulas. Essentially all production pricing is numerical, and choosing the wrong method costs orders of magnitude in either accuracy or time.

The intuition

Feynman–Kac gave you two equivalent descriptions of the price. It turns out they're also two algorithms, with opposite strengths — and the choice between them is almost entirely determined by two questions: how many state variables, and is there early exercise?

Monte Carlo takes the expectation seriously. Simulate a large number of paths under \(\mathbb{Q}\), evaluate the payoff on each, average, discount. Its defining property is the error, \(\varepsilon\sim\sigma/\sqrt{N}\) — which is bad news and good news simultaneously. Bad: to add one decimal digit you need 100× the paths. Good: the \(\sqrt{N}\) has no dimension in it. A 50-asset basket costs the same per path as a 1-asset option. Monte Carlo is the only method that doesn't collapse in high dimensions, and it handles path-dependency for free because you have the whole path in hand.

Its weakness is early exercise. To decide whether to exercise at time \(t\), you need the continuation value — a conditional expectation of the future, at a point where your simulation only knows the future of this one path. Using it is peeking, and it violates adaptedness. Longstaff–Schwartz solves this by regressing continuation values on current state across paths, which is clever but adds bias and complexity.

Finite differences take the PDE seriously. Lay a grid over \((S,t)\), replace derivatives with differences, and march backward from the payoff. Early exercise is nearly free: at every node you already know the continuation value, so you just take the max with the intrinsic value. Greeks are free too — you have the whole solution surface, so delta and gamma are just differences of adjacent grid points, far more accurate than bumping and re-running a Monte Carlo. And convergence is \(O(\Delta t+\Delta S^2)\) or better, hugely faster than \(1/\sqrt{N}\).

The catch is dimension. A grid with 200 points per axis is 200 nodes in 1D, 40,000 in 2D, 8,000,000 in 3D, and hopeless in 5D. This is the curse of dimensionality, and it's the hard wall that sends you back to Monte Carlo.

Low dimension → PDE. High dimension → Monte Carlo. Early exercise pushes toward PDE; path dependence pushes toward Monte Carlo.

One more thing that distinguishes people who've done this from people who've read about it: variance reduction is not optional. Antithetic variates, control variates (price the exotic and a vanilla on the same paths, and correct using the vanilla's known closed form) and quasi-random sequences routinely buy 10–100× speedups. A control variate is often worth more than an order of magnitude more paths, and it's five lines of code.

The formal math

Monte Carlo. Simulate \(S^{(i)}_T\) under \(\mathbb{Q}\); the estimator and its error:

$$\hat V=\frac{e^{-rT}}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}h\big(S^{(i)}_T\big),\qquad \mathrm{s.e.}=\frac{e^{-rT}\hat\sigma_h}{\sqrt N},\qquad \text{95\% CI}=\hat V\pm1.96\,\mathrm{s.e.}$$

Exact GBM stepping (no discretisation error, because the SDE has a closed-form solution):

$$S_{t+\Delta t}=S_t\exp\Big[\big(r-\tfrac12\sigma^2\big)\Delta t+\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z\Big],\quad Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$$

For SDEs without closed forms (Heston, CIR), Euler–Maruyama has weak order 1 and strong order ½; Milstein adds a \(\tfrac12\sigma\sigma'(\Delta W^2-\Delta t)\) term to reach strong order 1 — and note that correction term is quadratic variation making yet another appearance.

Finite differences. Discretise the Black–Scholes PDE with \(V_i^n\approx V(n\Delta t,\,i\Delta S)\):

$$\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\approx\frac{V_{i+1}-V_{i-1}}{2\Delta S},\qquad \frac{\partial^2V}{\partial S^2}\approx\frac{V_{i+1}-2V_i+V_{i-1}}{\Delta S^2}$$

Crank–Nicolson can oscillate near the payoff kink; the standard fix is a few fully-implicit steps first (Rannacher smoothing).

Going deeper: variance reduction, quantified

Antithetic variates. Use \(Z\) and \(-Z\). Free, and helps whenever the payoff has a monotone component; typically 1.5–2× effective speedup.

Control variates. Price a related claim \(Y\) with known value \(\mathbb{E}[Y]\) on the same paths and use \(\hat V_{cv}=\hat V-\beta(\hat Y-\mathbb{E}[Y])\) with \(\beta=\mathrm{Cov}(V,Y)/\mathrm{Var}(Y)\). The residual variance is \((1-\rho^2)\mathrm{Var}(V)\), so a correlation of 0.99 cuts variance 50×. Pricing an Asian option with the geometric-average Asian (closed-form) as control is the textbook case, and it genuinely works that well.

Quasi-Monte Carlo. Sobol sequences fill space more evenly than pseudorandom draws, giving error closer to \(O(1/N)\) than \(O(1/\sqrt N)\) in effectively-low-dimensional problems. Requires Brownian bridge construction to concentrate the important dimensions first.

Importance sampling. For deep-OTM options almost every path contributes zero, so nearly all the work is wasted. Shift the drift to push paths into the money, then correct with the Radon–Nikodym derivative — which is Girsanov deployed as a computational trick rather than a pricing principle. Speedups of 100× or more in the far wings.

Common confusion

"Monte Carlo converges slowly, so it's the worse method." The \(1/\sqrt N\) rate is dimension-independent, and that's the whole point. Finite differences converge far faster in 1D and are simply unusable by 5D; Monte Carlo's rate is unchanged at 500 assets. Judge a method by cost-to-reach-a-tolerance in your problem's dimension, not by convergence order in the abstract. A related trap: reporting a Monte Carlo price without its standard error. An unqualified number implies precision the estimator doesn't have, and "the model says 4.17" has caused real losses when the honest statement was 4.17 ± 0.31.

Putting it back together

Why this matters

The nineteen concepts above compress considerably. Stated as a single chain of reasoning, the logical dependencies become visible in a way that the conventional topic-by-topic ordering obscures.

The whole thing, in one paragraph

Prices are expectations of payoffs. Because randomness in markets is continuous but infinitely jagged, ordinary calculus fails, and the repair — Itô — costs exactly one extra term, whose source is the single fact that squared Brownian increments accumulate to \(t\) instead of vanishing. Because you can manufacture an option by trading its underlying, its price is its manufacturing cost, which makes the price independent of anyone's beliefs about returns. Formalising that gives a fictional probability measure under which discounted prices are fair games; expectations under that measure solve a PDE; and evaluating that expectation for a lognormal stock and a call payoff gives Black–Scholes. Everything afterwards is the discovery that real markets have fat tails, random volatility, jumps and moving interest rates — and each fix is the same move: add a state variable, restore the Markov property, and re-run the argument.

Five questions the material resolves

  1. Why \(\mu\) is absent from the Black–Scholes formula. There are two derivations — one via hedging, one via Girsanov — and they are equivalent.
  2. Why Itô's lemma carries a second-order term where the chain rule does not.
  3. In what precise sense the Black–Scholes PDE and the \(N(d_1),N(d_2)\) formula are the same object.
  4. Which risks remain in a delta-hedged short option position: gamma, vega, theta, and gap risk.
  5. Why a downward skew admits at least two economically distinct causes — tail probability and variance risk premium — that option prices alone cannot separate.

Common confusion

Believing that more advanced models are more correct. They aren't more correct; they are wrong in more carefully chosen places. Black–Scholes is wrong about tails, Heston is wrong about short-dated skew, jump models are wrong about vol clustering, and every one of them is wrong about liquidity. There is no true model to select. What matters is knowing precisely which assumption each one sacrifices, which determines in advance where its prices will be wrong and by roughly how much.
