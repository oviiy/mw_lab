Big Tech has lost its borrowing discount

On 30-year bonds, Meta, Alphabet and Amazon went from paying 12 to 58 basis points less than the average investment-grade borrower to paying more. Their shareholders see almost no risk. Here is what that confidence rests on.

In short

Bond investors have not panicked about AI debt. They have done something quieter: they stopped giving the biggest technology companies a discount, and the change is largest on the longest loans.

On recent deals Meta, Alphabet and Amazon paid about what any AA-rated company pays for five-year money. For thirty-year money, the decades after the servers it pays for are written off, each now pays more than the average investment-grade bond, having paid less on its first AI-era deal.

Shareholders see it differently. Read through a standard credit model, these share prices imply almost no chance of default, because the companies' market values dwarf their debts. Much of that value arrived after ChatGPT. The stress lab lets you take it out and see what is left.

Key findings

  1. The five-year discount is gone. Alphabet's 5-year bonds went from 25 bp below the AA average to 3 bp above, Amazon's from 9 below to 2 above, Meta's from 9 below to 1 above. Oracle went from 45 bp below the BBB average to 22 above. Chapter 1
  2. The biggest swing is in 30-year money. Against the average long investment-grade bond, Alphabet's 30-year bond went from −58 to +16 bp, Amazon's from −27 to +14, Meta's from −12 to +34, Oracle's from +16 to +88. Chapter 2
  3. We can't say why. The borrowing, the AI stock rally and time all rose together; with 13 comparable deals they cannot be told apart. Chapter 2
  4. Shareholders see almost no risk, and much of their cushion arrived after ChatGPT. Net of the market, 63% of Meta's value was added since. Chapter 3
  5. Count the signed leases and remove the gains beyond the market, and Meta's market value would cover what it owes 1.8 times instead of 4.9. Alphabet's and Microsoft's would still cover it more than ten times. Chapter 4
Chapter 1

The discount is gone

The grey line is what an ordinary top-rated company pays to borrow: the average spread over Treasuries on bonds rated AA. It drifted down through 2025 as corporate credit got cheaper.

In April 2025, Alphabet sold 5-year bonds at 32 bp over Treasuries, 25 bp less than the AA average: investors accepted a lower return to hold Alphabet than other AA companies. By August 2026, after $86 billion more of borrowing, its 5-year bond paid 60 bp, 3 bp above the average.

Amazon followed the same path, from 9 bp below the AA average to 2 bp above. Meta's own 5-year spreads barely moved (55, 50 and 53 bp); what changed is that the average fell to meet them.

Oracle is compared with the BBB average, the rating band its bonds sit in. In September 2024 it borrowed 45 bp below that average. In February 2026 it paid 22 bp above.

Each deal is priced against one benchmark Treasury; the averages cover thousands of bonds. Read the direction, not single basis points. As a ratio of the average, the shift is the same: Alphabet from 0.56× to 1.05×, Oracle from 0.61× to 1.24×.

A discount like that is a scarcity premium. Before the AI build-out, bonds from these companies were rare and investors paid up for them. Two years of record-sized deals have made them common. Over five years, lenders are not charging these companies more than their rating suggests. They have simply stopped giving them a better deal.

Chapter 2

Paying for the decades after the chips wear out

The AI Debt Tracker found that 73% of this borrowing falls due after the servers it paid for have been written off. Servers last five or six years. Meta, Alphabet, Amazon and Oracle have all sold 30- and 40-year bonds, and Alphabet a 100-year one. That is where the price has moved most.

Pick a company and a deal. Each dot is one bond at its maturity, sized by amount. The grey curve is what the average investment-grade bond paid at each maturity that day.

Meta's first 30-year bond, in August 2024, paid 115 bp over Treasuries when the average long investment-grade bond paid 127. Its latest, in April 2026, paid 132 bp against 98. Alphabet's and Amazon's moved the same way. Their curves also got steeper than the market's: the extra a 30-year bond paid over the same company's 5-year, beyond what the market charged for that stretch, rose from 29 to 53 bp for Meta, 6 to 23 for Alphabet, 8 to 24 for Amazon and 28 to 38 for Oracle.

Lenders are relaxed about the next five years. They want to be paid for the decades after the chips are gone.

Was it the AI frenzy?

It is tempting to say so. But three things rose together over the same two years: the borrowing, Nvidia's lead over the market (it has returned 6.5 times as much as the S&P 500 since ChatGPT), and the price of these bonds against their peers. With 13 comparable deals there is no way to tell which one moved bond prices, or whether time alone did.

What we can say is narrower. The repricing happened during the largest borrowing spree these companies have ever undertaken, and it has not reversed. Whether the cause is the AI story, the volume of bonds, or both, bond investors have stopped treating these companies as special. The tests are in the method notes.

Chapter 3

Shareholders see almost no risk

Share prices carry their own view of default. Shareholders are paid only after lenders, so a share price is a bet that the company's assets will stay worth more than its debts. From the market value of the shares, how much they swing and how much the company owes, a standard credit model works out how likely the assets are to fall below the debt, and what a lender should charge for that risk.

For Alphabet, Amazon and Microsoft the line sits on the floor, under 1 bp. Meta's is 1 bp. Their market values are many times their debts, so even a deep fall in the shares would leave lenders whole. Oracle is the exception; Chapter 6 returns to it.

That cushion is what bondholders are leaning on. Figure 5 splits each company's market value into what it was worth on 30 November 2022, the day ChatGPT launched, what it would be worth had its shares only kept pace with the S&P 500 since, and the rest.

Net of the market, 63% of Meta's value was added after ChatGPT, 38% of Alphabet's, 23% of Amazon's and 8% of Microsoft's. Not all of that is AI: Meta's earnings recovered sharply from a 2022 low. But it is the part of the cushion that depends on the market continuing to believe these companies will out-earn everyone else.

Chapter 4

Stress lab: take the rally out

Choose a company, a share price and what counts as owed ahead of shareholders. The model recomputes the spread that share price would imply. By default the business is assumed to stay as volatile as it is today, so a lower share price always means more risk; you can change that under Advanced.

Try Meta at "had it only matched the S&P" with its signed leases counted. Its market value would cover what it owes 1.8 times rather than 4.9 times today, and its model spread rises further than any other hyperscaler's. Alphabet and Microsoft barely move under the same settings: their market values would still cover their claims 12.2 and 10.9 times over.

Chapter 5

$1.15 trillion below the waterline

The bonds are the visible part. Below them sit data-center leases the companies have signed but which have not started, so they are not yet on any balance sheet. They are disclosed in the notes to each quarterly report and add up to 3 times the bonds tracked since 2024. As each lease starts, it becomes a liability much like debt.

Microsoft has sold no bonds since 2024, which is why the Debt Tracker leaves it out, yet it has signed $329 billion of leases that have not started, more than any company here. Meta's $347 billion, including $68 billion signed after its June quarter, exceeds its entire stock-market value on the day ChatGPT launched ($303 billion). Rating agencies already treat leases as debt-like. The question for bondholders is how much of this wave is in the price.

Chapter 6

Where shareholders already agree: Oracle and CoreWeave

Oracle has become an AI stock: its shares now move with Nvidia's, which two years ago they did not. The spread its share price implies was near zero until mid-2025, reached 135 bp by the end of 2025 and peaked at 227 bp in July 2026. It is 152 bp today, above the 115 bp its last 5-year bond paid in February 2026, nine months ago. Here the stock market came round to the bond market's view.

CoreWeave, in brief

CoreWeave has no investment-grade rating, and its unsecured notes have paid 506 to 584 bp over Treasuries. Its share price implies more: 558 bp, from a $29 billion market value against $45 billion of debt and shares that swing 95% a year. For the most leveraged company here, the stock is the more worried of the two markets. Much of its borrowing is secured on its GPUs, so its lenders rely on collateral more than on an equity cushion.

Method

How this was made

Everything here is built from public data. Nothing proprietary was used.

Bond prices

92 USD fixed-rate senior notes from 14 investment-grade deals and CoreWeave's notes, from SEC pricing term sheets as extracted and checked by the AI Debt Tracker (version 2026.10.15). Spreads are as stated on the term sheet, against a single benchmark Treasury. No maturity adjustment: bonds are compared at the maturity they were sold. "5-year" means tranches of 4.5 to 6.6 years; "30-year" means the tranche closest to 30 years among those of 25 to 35, which every first and latest deal has. Rating comparison: ICE BofA US Corporate AA and BBB indices, which span all maturities. Maturity comparison: ICE BofA US Corporate 1–3, 3–5, 5–7, 7–10, 10–15 and 15+ year indices, which span all investment-grade ratings; the 15+ index averages about 20 years to maturity, shorter than a 30-year bond, so its level understates what a 30-year bond would pay. All are option-adjusted spreads from FRED on the pricing date. Rating bands are approximate and held fixed over the period. The first deals for Meta (7 August 2024) and Alphabet (28 April 2025) priced during market sell-offs, which widens the index and exaggerates the starting discount; ratios to the index show the same direction.

What the data can and cannot separate

Deal-level regressions of the 30-year curve premium (30-year minus 5-year spread, less the market's 15+ minus 3–5 year spread) on a separate intercept per company plus one variable at a time, 13 deals with both tranches, ordinary standard errors: a time trend gives R² 0.72; prior borrowing 23 bp per $100B (p = 0.02); Nvidia relative to the S&P 500 2.5 bp per unit (p = 0.54). With time added, borrowing falls to 18 bp (s.e. 12) and Nvidia to -6.3 bp. Tranche level (87 bonds, clustered by deal): the spread over the same-maturity index rises 8 bp per doubling of maturity, and the change in 2026 (2 bp per doubling, s.e. 3) is too small to tell apart from noise. Each company's comparison rests on its first and latest deals; with three or four deals each, treat the direction as solid and the size as approximate.

Share-price model

Merton model, solved weekly for asset value and asset volatility from market value (close × diluted weighted shares), trailing 252-day share-price volatility (at least 120 days) and the default point: reported total debt (the most comprehensive XBRL tag, with zero treated as missing; tags describing single bond issues are never used), plus finance leases, plus bonds and loans priced since the last reported quarter. Oracle reports a debt total only once a year, so its debt between reports is the last total plus new borrowing. The five-year risk-neutral default probability is converted to a spread with a 60% loss given default. Scenarios hold asset volatility at today's calibrated level unless changed, and solve for the asset value consistent with the scenario's share price. Counting long leases in full against a five-year horizon is aggressive; credit-desk practice often counts half of long-term obligations.

Limits

Few deals, all in one market regime. No free secondary-market bond prices (TRACE) or CDS quotes, so new-issue concessions cannot be separated from a repricing of bonds already outstanding. Lease commitments are disclosed as totals, not schedules. Meta's Hyperion venture exposure may overlap with its lease commitments, so it is shown but not added. Volatility is backward-looking.

Review and corrections

The analysis was read before publication by an independent credit-markets referee and a markets editor, in two rounds each. Their review led to: rating- and maturity-matched benchmarks in place of the all-investment-grade index; no maturity adjustment; a fixed 30-year tenor in place of each deal's longest bond; withdrawing a draft estimate of the effect of borrowing that was a time trend; two fixes to Oracle's debt series; value added measured net of the S&P 500's total return; stress scenarios that respond to leverage; and no rating comparisons on model output.
