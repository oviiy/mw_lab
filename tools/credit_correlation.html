Loss distribution

ρ reshapes how 125 fixed default probabilities combine into a portfolio loss — it cannot move their sum.

How this works

A one-factor Gaussian copula over the real rating ladder: all 125 names default when their own latent factor crosses their own hazard-implied threshold, and ρ is the pairwise correlation of those latent factors. The date slider above redraws every name's five-year default probability from that day's OAS; ρ above only reshapes how 125 fixed probabilities combine into a portfolio loss — it cannot, by construction, move their sum. That invariance is what the highlighted cell in the readout below is for, and it is the same invariance the rail to the left is drawn to make visible without reading a number: drag ρ and its bands redistribute while the waterline holds still.

Assumptions and the OAS caveat

Assumptions, not quotes: the 125-name pool (1 AAA / 5 AA / 44 A / 73 BBB / 2 BB) and the recovery rate set above are both modelling choices, not market-observed facts — there is no public source that hands you a reference portfolio's exact rating composition. And the whole OAS above is being attributed to default risk, the risk-neutral reading, which is why the mean 5y PD in the readout (≈7%) runs several times the historical default rate for a BBB-heavy pool: it is pricing default risk plus everything else priced into a credit spread — liquidity, risk premia, uncertainty — as if it were default risk alone.

Why compound correlation breaks

Where inversion fails

Compound correlation bisects ρ against a quote — but tranche E[L] is not monotone in ρ, so that bisection can have two roots or none.

Why the mezzanine tranche fails to invert

Compound correlation prices a tranche [a,d] by bisecting a single ρ against a quoted expected loss. That only works if tranche E[L] is monotone in ρ — and it is not, in general. At ρ = 0 the 125-name loss distribution concentrates tightly around pool E[L] (idiosyncratic risk diversifies away): a tranche that detaches at or below that concentration point is an almost-sure loss, so raising ρ can only help it (spreading the distribution pulls some of that mass past the detachment, back to the senior side); a tranche that attaches well above it is an almost-sure survivor, so raising ρ can only hurt it (spreading the distribution is what reaches up into that tranche at all). A tranche that straddles the concentration point, or sits just above it, is pulled both ways at once — long ρ near its attachment, short ρ near its detachment — and its E[L] curve rises then falls. That is not "the mezzanine tranche": it is whichever tranche the concentration point happens to sit inside or just below at a given date, which is why the date slider above moves which tranche fails to invert — and which band the rail marks. The 2005 CDX mezz blowup is this mechanism, not a modelling accident: a fixed 3–7% tranche's compound-correlation quote briefly had two solutions, then none, as spreads moved the crossover band across it.

Why base correlation inverts uniquely

Every tranche [a,d] is the difference of two equity tranches, (d·E[min(L,d)]/d − a·E[min(L,a)]/a) / (d − a), each individually monotone. That change of coordinates is why the base table's two highlighted rows above always return exactly one root (or an honest "unidentifiable", never two, never zero) for the same quote that the compound panel can leave with two solutions or none.

The fix, and how to break it

Break the base curve

A base curve implies E[min(L,K)]; break its increasing-and-concave shape and some tranche implies negative loss.

Why concavity is the arbitrage condition

A base-correlation curve assigns one ρ to every detachment K, and that assignment implies E[min(L,K)], the expected loss of the [0,K] equity tranche, as a function of K. For any genuine loss distribution that function must be increasing (more principal at risk cannot lower expected loss) and concave (its slope, the survival probability P(L>K), can only fall as K rises — the second difference of E[min(L,K)] is minus the loss density). Break concavity and the curve has implied a negative density: some tranche along it carries negative expected loss. This is g(k) ≥ 0 from the arbitrage-free surface lab wearing a different hat. What the market is pricing now

Dispersion through time

CCC−BB dispersion is correlation's observable shadow: how concentrated the pool's risk sits in a handful of weak names.

Why dispersion is correlation's shadow

Every other section prices tranches off a single date's seven-point rating ladder. This one asks what the real three-year history of that ladder says about the correlation a tranche market is implicitly pricing. Cross-sectional dispersion — how far the rating buckets have pulled apart from one another, CCC and BB in particular — is the observable shadow of correlation: wide dispersion against a benign investment-grade index means the pool's risk sits concentrated in a handful of weak names rather than spread evenly across all 125, which is exactly the idiosyncratic, undiversified risk an equity-tranche buyer is long and a senior-tranche buyer is insulated from. The index level alone cannot tell you that; the gap between buckets can.
